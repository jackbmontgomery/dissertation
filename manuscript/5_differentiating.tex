\chapter{Differentiating Through the Forward Solver}\label{ch:adjoint}

Computing the gradient $\nabla_\phi \ell$ of the
log-likelihood~\cref{eq:log-likelihood} requires differentiating through
the forward map~$\mathcal{P}$, which involves solving a sequence of
tridiagonal linear systems. In this chapter we derive an efficient adjoint
method for this computation and demonstrate its value through gradient-based
optimisation.

\section{Discretise Then Optimise}\label{sec:disc-then-opt}

There are two strategies for differentiating through a numerical solver.
The \emph{optimise then discretise} approach derives continuous adjoint
equations and then discretises them; the \emph{discretise then optimise}
approach differentiates the discrete computation directly. We adopt the
latter, which yields the exact gradient of the quantity evaluated in code
and avoids consistency errors between the forward and adjoint
discretisations~\cite{kidger2021neural}.

\section{Reverse-Mode AD and the Cost of Na\"ive Taping}%
\label{sec:reverse-ad}

Since $\ell$ is scalar-valued, reverse-mode automatic differentiation
(AD) computes the full gradient in a single backward pass. It
decomposes the computation into a sequence of
\emph{vector--Jacobian products} (VJPs).

For $f = f_L \circ \cdots \circ f_1$, the chain rule gives
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial f}{\partial \phi}
  = J_{f_L}\, J_{f_{L-1}} \cdots J_{f_1},
  \label{eq:chain-rule}
\end{equation}
which reverse mode AD evaluates from left to right, multiplying the
cotangent vector by each Jacobian in turn without forming the
intermediate matrices explicitly. In the present setting $f = \mathcal{P}$.

The dominant operations in $\mathcal{P}$ are the $m$ tridiagonal solves, so the
central question is how to implement the VJP of a linear solve $Ax =
b$. A generic AD framework will tape every intermediate value of the
Thomas algorithm -- elimination multipliers, modified diagonals,
partial sums -- and replay them in reverse. The overhead of
maintaining and traversing the tape within the linear solve can
degrade runtime performance significantly.

\section{Adjoint of the Tridiagonal Solve}\label{sec:adjoint-solve}

We avoid taping by exploiting the structure of the linear solve. Given
the forward system $Ax = b$ and a scalar loss $\ell$, the task is to
express the adjoints $\bar{b} = \partial \ell / \partial b$ and
$\bar{A} = \partial \ell / \partial A$ in terms of the incoming
cotangent $\bar{x} = \partial \ell / \partial x$.

Define $\lambda$ by the \emph{adjoint system}
\begin{equation}
  A^T \lambda = \bar{x}.
  \label{eq:adjoint-system}
\end{equation}
Differentiating $Ax = b$ and allowing both $A$ and $b$ to vary gives
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{d}A\, x + A\, \mathrm{d}x = \mathrm{d}b,
  \label{eq:total-diff}
\end{equation}
Left-multiplying~\cref{eq:total-diff} by $\lambda^T$ and
applying~\cref{eq:adjoint-system}
\begin{equation}
  \lambda^T\, \mathrm{d}A\, x + \bar{x}^T \mathrm{d}x
  = \lambda^T\, \mathrm{d}b,
\end{equation}
by rearranging and noting $\mathrm{d}\ell = \bar{x}^T \mathrm{d}x$
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{d}\ell
  = \bar{x}^T \mathrm{d}x
  = \lambda^T\, \mathrm{d}b - \lambda^T\, \mathrm{d}A\, x.
  \label{eq:dloss}
\end{equation}
Reading off the coefficients of $\mathrm{d}b$ and $\mathrm{d}A$ identifies the
adjoints:
\begin{equation}
  \bar{b} = \lambda^T,
  \qquad
  \bar{A} = -\lambda\, x^T.
  \label{eq:adjoints}
\end{equation}

Two observations make this practical. First, $A^T$ is tridiagonal, so
the adjoint system~\cref{eq:adjoint-system} is solved by the Thomas
algorithm at the same $O(n)$ cost as the forward solve. Second,
$\bar{A}$ is never formed as a $n \times n$ matrix: only the
three diagonals are needed, and
$\bar{A}_{ij} = -\lambda_i x_j$ on each diagonal reduces to the
elementwise products
\begin{equation}
  \bar{a}_i = -\lambda_{i+1}\, x_i, \quad
  \bar{b}_i^{\text{diag}} = -\lambda_i\, x_i, \quad
  \bar{c}_i = -\lambda_i\, x_{i+1},
  \label{eq:diag-adjoints}
\end{equation}
for the sub-, main- and super-diagonals of $A$.

\Cref{ch:appendix-adjoint} lists the JAX~\cite{jax2018github}
implementation. The solver takes the three diagonals $a, b, c$ and
right-hand side $d$ as separate arrays. The forward pass returns $x$
and caches $(a, b, c, x)$. The backward pass solves the adjoint
system by calling the same Thomas routine with the sub- and
super-diagonals swapped and returns the three diagonal adjoints
from~\cref{eq:diag-adjoints} together with $\bar{d} = \lambda$.

\subsection{Full Backward Pass}\label{sec:full-backward}

The custom VJP handles a single timestep. The full gradient
$\nabla_\phi \ell$ is assembled by walking backward through time levels
$k = m, m-1, \ldots, 1$. At each level the adjoint
system~\cref{eq:adjoint-system} is solved using the cached $A^k$ diagonals
and~$x^k$, yielding $\lambda^k$ and the matrix adjoint
$\bar{A}^k = -\lambda^k (x^k)^T$. Only the first row of $A^k$ depends
on $\phi$ through $K_{\mathrm{red}}$ and $K_{\mathrm{ox}}$. The
interior rows are parameter-independent, so
the contributions to $\nabla_\phi \ell$ come exclusively from $\bar{A}^k$
applied to the partial derivatives $\partial A^k_{0,\cdot} / \partial
\phi$. The result is the exact gradient of the discrete
log-likelihood at a cost of one adjoint solve per timestep.

The asymptotic complexity of the backward pass is unchanged: both
na\"ive taping and the adjoint require $O(mn)$ work, since each of the
$m$ timesteps needs one $O(n)$ backward solve. The saving is in the
memory constant and in eliminating tape-traversal overhead. For the
electron reaction this reduces the backward pass from
$15.8$\,ms with standard AD to $7.25$\,ms with the custom rule.

\section{Extension to Pentadiagonal Systems}\label{sec:pentadiagonal}

The adjoint generalises to pentadiagonal systems: the transpose
of such a matrix retains the same band structure, so the adjoint system
is solved at the same $O(n)$ cost per timestep. We exploit this in
\cref{ch:heterogeneous,ch:adsorption}, where coupled multi-species
reactions lead to pentadiagonal structure. The saving grows with
bandwidth. For the heterogeneous reaction of \cref{ch:heterogeneous}
(pentadiagonal, 7 parameters) the backward pass takes $43.7$\,ms with
the custom VJP against $481$\,ms for standard AD. This more than
$10\times$ reduction in the backward pass makes scaling to wider-band
problems practical within our runtime budget.

\section{Optimisation Results}\label{sec:init-results}

We now use the adjoint gradient to locate the high-density region of
the posterior and compare the gradient-based ADAM
optimiser~\cite{deepmind2020jax,kingma2014adam} against
CMA-ES~\cite{hansen2001evolution,evosax2022github}, a gradient-free
evolutionary strategy that adapts a population covariance over
generations, used in Gavaghan et~al.~\cite{gavaghan2018bayesian}.

A single ADAM step requires one forward and one adjoint
solve, costing approximately two forward-solve equivalents. We use
the recommended CMA-ES population size of $\lambda = 4 + \lfloor 3
\ln d\rfloor = 7$~\cite{hansen2001evolution}, requiring $\lambda$
forward solves per generation. We equalise the total budget in
forward-solve equivalents so that both methods consume the same
runtime.

\Cref{fig:electron-optim} shows the log-posterior density over the
course of optimisation at two noise levels ($\eta = 0.01$ and
$\eta = 0.02$), each run from $32$ initial conditions drawn by Latin
hypercube sampling centred at the true parameters. ADAM reaches the
high-density threshold (dashed line) in fewer forward-solve
equivalents and with lower variance across initialisations. In three
dimensions both methods reach the region reliably, so the gradient
advantage is marginal; it becomes important in higher dimensions
(\cref{ch:appendix-optim}).
