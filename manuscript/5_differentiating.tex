\chapter{Differentiating Through the Forward Solver}\label{ch:adjoint}

The previous chapter established that gradient information can both
initialise the Markov chain near the posterior mode and enable more
efficient MCMC proposals. Computing the gradient of the
log-likelihood~\cref{eq:log-likelihood} with respect to the
parameters~$\phi$ requires differentiating through the forward
map~$\mathcal{P}$, which itself involves solving a sequence of tridiagonal
linear systems. In this chapter we describe how these derivatives are
obtained efficiently, demonstrate their value through gradient-based
optimisation, and compare the result against a gradient-free alternative.

\section{Discretise Then Optimise}\label{sec:disc-then-opt}

There are broadly two strategies for differentiating through a numerical
solver. In the \emph{optimise then discretise} approach one derives the
continuous adjoint equations analytically and then discretises them; in the
\emph{discretise then optimise} approach one first discretises the forward
problem and then differentiates the discrete computation. The latter is
preferable here because it computes the exact gradient of the quantity
that is actually evaluated in code, avoiding the consistency errors that
can arise when the adjoint PDE is discretised independently of the forward
PDE~\cite{kidger2021neural}. We adopt this approach throughout.

Concretely, our forward map~$\mathcal{P}$ is implemented as a
sequence of algebraic operations --- assembling tridiagonal systems and
solving them via the Thomas algorithm at each timestep, then extracting
the surface flux. The gradient we seek is therefore the derivative of a
composition of these discrete operations with respect to the
parameters~$\phi$.

\section{Reverse-Mode Automatic Differentiation}\label{sec:reverse-ad}

The log-likelihood is a scalar-valued function of the parameter
vector. In this setting reverse-mode automatic differentiation (AD) is
the natural choice, as it computes the full gradient in a single backward
pass regardless of the number of parameters.

Reverse-mode AD decomposes the gradient computation into a sequence of
\emph{vector--Jacobian products} (VJPs). If the forward computation is
written as a composition $f = f_L \circ f_{L-1} \circ \cdots \circ f_1$,
then the chain rule gives
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial f}{\partial \phi}
  = J_{f_L}\, J_{f_{L-1}} \cdots J_{f_1},
  \label{eq:chain-rule}
\end{equation}
where $J_{f_i}$ denotes the Jacobian matrix of~$f_i$ with respect to its
input. In reverse mode this product is evaluated from left to right: at
each stage a row vector (the adjoint, or cotangent) is multiplied by the
Jacobian of the corresponding primitive, so that the intermediate
Jacobians are never formed explicitly. Only the VJP of each primitive is
required.

For the forward map~$\mathcal{P}$ the dominant primitives are the
tridiagonal solves --- one per timestep. The question therefore reduces
to: how should the VJP of a tridiagonal solve be implemented?

\section{Na\"ive Taping and Its Cost}\label{sec:naive-taping}

Consider a single linear system $A x = b$, where $A$ depends on the
parameters~$\phi$ and $b$ depends on the solution at the previous
timestep. A na\"ive approach is to record every intermediate value of the
Thomas algorithm on the forward pass and replay them in reverse during the
backward pass. This is what a generic AD framework will do if the Thomas
algorithm is implemented as a sequence of elementary operations: the
entire computational graph of the factorisation and back-substitution is
stored on a tape.

For a single tridiagonal solve on $n$ unknowns the memory overhead of
taping is modest. However, the forward map involves $m$ consecutive
solves (one per timestep), and the tape must be retained across all of
them because the backward pass traverses the full time history in
reverse. The total memory cost therefore scales as $O(mn)$, which becomes
prohibitive for fine spatial and temporal grids. The overhead of
maintaining and traversing the tape also degrades runtime performance even
when memory is not the binding constraint.

\section{Adjoint of the Tridiagonal Solve}\label{sec:adjoint-solve}

A more efficient approach exploits the structure of the linear solve
directly. Suppose the forward pass solves $Ax = b$ and the backward pass
receives the adjoint (cotangent) $\bar{x} = \partial \ell / \partial x$.
We require the adjoint with respect to~$b$:
\begin{equation}
  \bar{b} = \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial b}
  = \bar{x}^T \frac{\partial x}{\partial b}
  = \bar{x}^T A^{-1}.
  \label{eq:adjoint-b}
\end{equation}
This is equivalent to solving the \emph{adjoint system}
\begin{equation}
  A^T \lambda = \bar{x},
  \label{eq:adjoint-system}
\end{equation}
and setting $\bar{b} = \lambda^T$. To obtain the adjoint with respect to
the matrix entries of~$A$, we differentiate the identity $Ax = b$ to get
\begin{equation}
  dA\, x + A\, dx = db.
\end{equation}
Contracting both sides with the adjoint variable~$\lambda^T$ (i.e.\
left-multiplying by $\lambda^T$) and using $\lambda^T A = \bar{x}^T$
gives
\begin{equation}
  \lambda^T dA\, x + \bar{x}^T dx = \lambda^T db.
\end{equation}
The term $\bar{x}^T dx$ contributes to $\bar{b}$, which has already been
accounted for. Reading off the remaining term, the adjoint with respect
to~$A$ is
\begin{equation}
  \bar{A} = -\lambda\, x^T,
  \label{eq:adjoint-A}
\end{equation}
where the entry $\bar{A}_{ij} = -\lambda_i\, x_j$.

The key observation is that $A^T$ is also tridiagonal (with the sub- and
super-diagonals transposed), so the adjoint
system~\cref{eq:adjoint-system} can be solved by the Thomas algorithm at
the same $O(n)$ cost as the forward solve. This approach requires only
the matrix~$A$ and the forward solution~$x$ to be cached per timestep ---
not the full tape of intermediate values from the factorisation. The tape
for na\"ive AD records every intermediate scalar produced during the
Thomas algorithm (elimination multipliers, modified diagonals, partial
sums), whereas the adjoint method stores only two arrays of length~$n$
per timestep. The total storage across all~$m$ timesteps is $O(mn)$ in
both cases, but the per-timestep constant is reduced from the number of
intermediate operations in the Thomas algorithm to just the system matrix
and solution vector.

In practice we implement this by defining a custom VJP rule for the
tridiagonal solve in JAX~\cite{jax2018github}. On the forward pass
the Thomas algorithm solves $Ax = b$ and caches $A$ and~$x$. On the
backward pass the custom rule receives~$\bar{x}$, solves
$A^T \lambda = \bar{x}$ using the banded transpose, and returns
$\bar{b} = \lambda^T$ and $\bar{A} = -\lambda\, x^T$. No taping of the
Thomas algorithm's internal operations is required.

\subsection{Full Backward Pass}\label{sec:full-backward}

The custom VJP handles a single timestep. The full gradient
$\nabla_\phi \ell$ is assembled by walking backward through the time
levels $k = m, m-1, \ldots, 1$. At each level the adjoint
system~\cref{eq:adjoint-system} is solved using the cached $A^k$
and~$x^k$, yielding $\lambda^k$. The matrix adjoint
$\bar{A}^k = -\lambda^k (x^k)^T$ is then propagated through the assembly
of~$A^k$ to obtain contributions to~$\nabla_\phi \ell$: the
Butler--Volmer coefficients in the first row depend on~$\phi$ through
$K_{\mathrm{red}}$ and $K_{\mathrm{ox}}$, so their partial derivatives
with respect to~$\alpha$, $K_0$, and~$\theta_f$ are accumulated at each
timestep. The interior coefficients are independent of~$\phi$ and
contribute nothing. The result is the exact gradient of the discrete
log-likelihood at a cost of one adjoint solve per timestep.

\section{Extension to Pentadiagonal Systems}\label{sec:pentadiagonal}

The custom adjoint generalises directly to pentadiagonal systems. The
transpose of a pentadiagonal matrix is again pentadiagonal, so the
adjoint system is solved by a banded solver at the same $O(n)$ cost per
timestep. The saving over na\"ive taping grows with bandwidth, since a
pentadiagonal factorisation produces more intermediate values than a
tridiagonal one. We exploit this in \cref{ch:heterogeneous,ch:adsorption},
where coupled multi-species reactions lead to pentadiagonal structure
after a spatial reordering of the unknowns.

\section{Optimisation Results}\label{sec:init-results}

As discussed in \cref{sec:sampling-results}, initialising a Markov chain
far from the posterior mode wastes computational resources on burn-in
samples that must be discarded. A gradient-based optimisation stage can
locate the high-density region before sampling begins, so that the chain
starts collecting useful draws immediately. A good initialisation
strategy should satisfy three criteria: it should be robust to the choice
of starting point, it should perform reliably across different noise
levels in the data, and it should remain stable once it has reached a
region of high likelihood rather than oscillating away from it.

We compare the ADAM optimiser~\cite{kingma2014adam}, which exploits the
gradient of the log-likelihood computed via the adjoint method, against
CMA-ES~\cite{hansen2001completely}, a gradient-free evolutionary strategy.
CMA-ES maintains a population of candidate solutions whose covariance is
adapted over generations. We use the default population size
$\lambda = 4 + \lfloor 3 \ln d \rfloor$~\cite{hansen2001completely},
which gives $\lambda = 7$ for the $d = 3$ parameter space of the
electron-transfer reaction. Since the backward (adjoint) pass costs
approximately $1$--$3\times$ the forward pass, a single ADAM step
(one forward and one backward pass) costs roughly two forward-solve
equivalents. One CMA-ES generation costs $\lambda = 7$ forward solves.
We fix a total budget of~$B$ forward-solve equivalents and
allocate $B / 2$ iterations to ADAM and $\lfloor B / 7 \rfloor$
generations to CMA-ES, so that both algorithms consume the same total
compute.

\Cref{fig:electron-optim} shows the log-posterior density over the course
of the optimisation at two noise levels ($\eta = 0.01$ and
$\eta = 0.02$). Each algorithm is run from $32$~initial conditions
drawn uniformly from a physically reasonable region of the parameter
space; solid lines show the mean and shaded regions indicate one standard
deviation across these runs. Both methods converge toward the posterior
mode, but ADAM reaches the high-density region in fewer forward-solve
equivalents and with lower variance across initialisations. At
$\eta = 0.02$, all $32$ ADAM runs are within $5\%$ of the modal
log-density by iteration~$100$, whereas several CMA-ES runs have not yet
converged at the same budget. Once ADAM reaches the mode it remains
stable, confirming its suitability as an initialisation strategy for the
sampler introduced in the next chapter.
