\chapter{Numerical Methods}\label{ch:numerical-methods}

In this chapter we discretise the dimensionless initial-boundary value
problem derived in the previous chapter and describe how the resulting
linear system is solved at each timestep. The reader is assumed to be
familiar with finite difference methods; we focus on the aspects specific
to the electrochemical setting, namely the exponentially expanding spatial
mesh and the incorporation of the Butler--Volmer boundary condition into
the tridiagonal system.

For the remainder of this chapter we assume $C_B^* = 0$ (no product
initially present in solution), so that the equal-diffusion-coefficient
boundary condition~\cref{eq:equal_diff_bc} becomes
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial C}{\partial X}\bigg|_{X=0} =
  K_{\mathrm{red}}(T)\, C(T,0)
  - K_{\mathrm{ox}}(T)\, \big(1 - C(T,0)\big),
  \label{eq:num_bc}
\end{equation}
where $K_{\mathrm{red}}$ and $K_{\mathrm{ox}}$ were defined
in~\cref{eq:kred_kox} and depend on time through the applied
potential~$\theta(T)$.

\section{Discretisation}\label{sec:discretisation}

\subsection{Temporal Grid}

We divide the simulation interval $[0, T_{\mathrm{max}}]$ into $m$
uniform steps of size
\begin{equation}
  \Delta T = \frac{T_{\mathrm{max}}}{m},
\end{equation}
so that the discrete time levels are $T^k = k\,\Delta T$ for
$k = 0, 1, \ldots, m$. Here $T_{\mathrm{max}}$ is determined by the
scan rate and the potential window of the experiment: for a cyclic
voltammogram sweeping from~$\theta_i$ to~$\theta_v$ and back,
$T_{\mathrm{max}} = \frac{2|\theta_v - \theta_i|}{\sigma}$.

\subsection{Spatial Grid}

The electrochemical reaction occurs at the electrode surface and its
influence on the concentration profile is confined to a thin diffusion
layer near $X = 0$. Far from the electrode the concentration remains at
its bulk value and contributes little to the dynamics. It is therefore
desirable to concentrate spatial resolution near the electrode surface
whilst using coarser spacing in the far field. We achieve this with an
exponentially expanding mesh controlled by two parameters: the initial
spacing $h_0 > 0$ and an expansion factor $\omega > 1$.

We define $n$ spatial nodes $\{X_0, X_1, \ldots, X_{n-1}\}$ by
\begin{equation}
  X_i =
  \begin{cases}
    \displaystyle \sum_{k=0}^{i-1} h_0\,\omega^k, & 0 \leq i \leq n-2, \\[6pt]
    X_{\mathrm{max}},                               & i = n-1,
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:spatial_grid}
\end{equation}
with $X_0 = 0$ corresponding to the electrode surface. The local spacings
about point~$i$ are
\begin{equation}
  \Delta X_+ = X_{i+1} - X_i, \qquad
  \Delta X_- = X_i - X_{i-1}.
  \label{eq:local_spacings}
\end{equation}
For $0 \leq i \leq n-2$ the spacing grows geometrically as
$\Delta X_+ = h_0\,\omega^i$, so the mesh is finest at the electrode and
coarsens exponentially into the bulk. The final point $(i = n-1)$ is placed at
$X_{\mathrm{max}} = 6\sqrt{T_{\mathrm{max}}}$ as discussed in the
previous chapter, ensuring the far-field boundary does not influence the
solution.

\section{Finite Difference Scheme}\label{sec:fd-scheme}

We discretise the dimensionless diffusion
equation~\cref{eq:nd_diffusion} (with $d_j = 1$) using a backward Euler
(fully implicit) scheme in time and a three-point central difference
approximation for the second spatial derivative on the non-uniform mesh.

Writing $C_i^k \approx C(T^k, X_i)$, the scheme at an interior node
$1 \leq i \leq n - 2$ reads
\begin{equation}
  \frac{C_i^k - C_i^{k-1}}{\Delta T}
  = \frac{1}{\tfrac{1}{2}(\Delta X_+ + \Delta X_-)}
  \left(
    \frac{C_{i+1}^k - C_i^k}{\Delta X_+}
    - \frac{C_i^k - C_{i-1}^k}{\Delta X_-}
  \right).
  \label{eq:fd_stencil}
\end{equation}
The implicit treatment evaluates the spatial operator at the new time
level~$k$, which makes the scheme unconditionally stable and avoids a
restrictive CFL condition on the timestep.

\subsection{Interior Coefficients}

Rearranging~\cref{eq:fd_stencil} so that all unknowns at time level~$k$
appear on the left and the known value at level~$k-1$ appears on the
right, we obtain
\begin{equation}
  \alpha_i\, C_{i-1}^k + \beta_i\, C_i^k + \gamma_i\, C_{i+1}^k
  = C_i^{k-1},
  \qquad 1 \leq i \leq n-2,
  \label{eq:tridiag_interior}
\end{equation}
where the tridiagonal coefficients are
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    \alpha_i &= -\frac{2\,\Delta T}
    {\Delta X_-\,(\Delta X_+ + \Delta X_-)}, \\[4pt]
    \gamma_i &= -\frac{2\,\Delta T}
    {\Delta X_+\,(\Delta X_+ + \Delta X_-)}, \\[4pt]
    \beta_i  &= 1 - \alpha_i - \gamma_i.
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:interior_coeffs}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Electrode Boundary ($i = 0$)}

At the electrode surface we approximate the spatial gradient
in~\cref{eq:num_bc} with a two-point forward difference:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{C_1^k - C_0^k}{h_0}
  = K_{\mathrm{red}}^k\, C_0^k
  - K_{\mathrm{ox}}^k\,(1 - C_0^k),
  \label{eq:fd_electrode_raw}
\end{equation}
where $K_{\mathrm{red}}^k$ and $K_{\mathrm{ox}}^k$ denote the values
of $K_{\mathrm{red}}$ and $K_{\mathrm{ox}}$ evaluated at the potential
$\theta(T^k)$. Rearranging into the form
$\beta_0\, C_0^k + \gamma_0\, C_1^k = b_0$ gives
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    \beta_0  &= 1 + h_0\, K_{\mathrm{red}}^k + h_0\, K_{\mathrm{ox}}^k, \\
    \gamma_0 &= -1, \\
    b_0      &= h_0\, K_{\mathrm{ox}}^k.
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:electrode_coeffs}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Far-Field Boundary ($i = n-1$)}

The Dirichlet condition $C(T, X_{\mathrm{max}}) = 1$ is imposed directly:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    \beta_{n-1}  &= 1, \\
    b_{n-1}      &= 1,
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:farfield_coeffs}
\end{equation}
with $\alpha_{n-1} = \gamma_{n-1} = 0$.

\subsection{Solution of the Tridiagonal System}

At each time level~$k$ the discretisation
produces a tridiagonal linear system with lower diagonal
$(\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_{n-1})$, main diagonal
$(\beta_0, \ldots, \beta_{n-1})$, and upper diagonal
$(\gamma_0, \ldots, \gamma_{n-2})$. Because the electrode boundary
coefficients depend on the applied potential, the first row of the system
is reassembled at each timestep; the interior coefficients are
time-independent and can be precomputed.

This system is solved in $O(n)$ operations using the Thomas algorithm
(tridiagonal LU factorisation), making the cost per timestep linear in
the number of spatial nodes.

\section{The Forward Solver as a Mapping}\label{sec:forward-map}

The implicit finite difference solver computes the concentration field at
every node in the spatial--temporal mesh. However, the experimentally
observable quantity is not the concentration profile but the current,
which is proportional to the surface flux~$J_{0,A}$ recorded at each
timestep. It is therefore natural to regard the solver as a mapping from
the set of model parameters to the predicted dimensionless current
response.

We define the \emph{forward map}
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{P} \colon \Phi \to \mathcal{J}, \qquad
  \phi \mapsto \mathcal{P}(\phi),
\end{equation}
where $\Phi \subseteq \mathbb{R}^3$ is the parameter space with
$\phi = (\alpha,\, K_0,\, \theta_f)$, and $\mathcal{J}$ is the space of
dimensionless flux trajectories $J_{0,A}(T)$ evaluated at the discrete
time levels. Given a parameter vector~$\phi$, a single evaluation
of~$\mathcal{P}$ consists of assembling and solving the tridiagonal system
at each of the $m$~timesteps and extracting the surface flux at each step.

Although the electrode boundary condition is discretised with a two-point
approximation (to preserve the tridiagonal structure), the surface flux
used to compute the current is evaluated with a higher-order three-point
formula. Writing $h_1 = X_1 - X_0$ and $h_2 = X_2 - X_0$, we fit a
quadratic through $(X_0, C_0^k)$, $(X_1, C_1^k)$, $(X_2, C_2^k)$ and
differentiate at $X_0$ to obtain
\begin{equation}
  J_{0,A}^k
  = -\left(\frac{\partial C}{\partial X}\right)\bigg|_{X=0}
  \approx -\frac{h_2^2\,(C_1^k - C_0^k) - h_1^2\,(C_2^k - C_0^k)}
  {h_1\, h_2\,(h_2 - h_1)}.
  \label{eq:numerical_flux}
\end{equation}

We can verify the solution produced from the mapping against
analytical results for the position and value of the peak current in
the reversible reigime \cite{compton2014understanding}. According to
the non-dimensionalisation we described in \cref{ch:electrochem}.

\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    J_{\text{p,irre}} &= -0.496 \sqrt{\alpha\sigma}\\
    \theta_{\text{p,irre}} &= \frac{1}{\alpha}\left[\ln
    \left(\frac{K_0}{\sqrt{\alpha \sigma}}\right)-0.78\right]
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:electron-analytical}
\end{equation}

As we can see in \cref{fig:electron-analytical}, we have excellent
agreement between the analytical current and the numerical flux computed.

\section{Effect of Butler--Volmer Parameters}\label{sec:bv-effects}

In the electron-transfer reaction we have been discussing there are
three parameters of interest: the transfer coefficient~$\alpha$, the
dimensionless rate constant~$K_0$, and the dimensionless formal
potential~$\theta_f$. Each parameter has a distinct qualitative effect on
the shape of the voltammogram. We illustrate these effects by varying
each parameter in turn about a quasi-reversible baseline in
\cref{fig:elec-parameter-effect-quasi}.

Increasing the transfer coefficient~$\alpha$ enhances the cathodic peak
current whilst diminishing the anodic peak; decreasing~$\alpha$ has the
opposite effect. This asymmetry arises because~$\alpha$ weights the
reduction and oxidation exponentials in the Butler--Volmer equation: a
larger~$\alpha$ favours the cathodic branch at the expense of the anodic
branch. The dimensionless rate constant~$K_0$ has a symmetrising effect
on the voltammogram. As~$K_0$ increases, the cathodic and anodic peaks
sharpen, grow in magnitude, and move closer together, reflecting the
transition from kinetically limited to near-reversible behaviour.
Finally, the formal potential~$\theta_f$ translates the entire
voltammogram along the potential axis without altering the peak shapes,
since it simply shifts the point about which the overpotential is
measured.

  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/3-parameter-effect-quasi.png}
  \end{center}
  \caption{Effect of each Butler--Volmer parameter on the dimensionless
    voltammogram for a quasi-reversible reaction. In each panel one
    parameter is varied while the others are held fixed. Left:
    varying~$\alpha$ changes the relative heights of the cathodic and
    anodic peaks. Centre: increasing~$K_0$ symmetrises and sharpens the
    voltammogram. Right: varying~$\theta_f$ translates the peaks along
  the potential axis.}
  \label{fig:elec-parameter-effect-quasi}
\end{figure}

The interplay between these parameters is important to note. Because
changes in one parameter can produce features in the voltammogram that
partially resemble changes in another, there is potential for
compensating errors in parameter estimation. For instance, a rate
constant that is too small --- which broadens the peaks and shifts them
apart --- could be partially offset by an adjustment in the formal
potential that translates the peaks back towards the observed positions.
Such parameter correlations suggest that a point estimate obtained by
optimisation alone may not capture the full uncertainty in the inferred
values.

The situation is compounded in the reversible limit.
\Cref{fig:alpha-parameter-effect-rev} shows the effect of varying
$\alpha$ at $K_0 = 200$, well into the reversible regime. The three
voltammograms for $\alpha = 0.3,\, 0.5,\, 0.7$ are nearly
indistinguishable: when the rate constant is large the exponential terms
in the Butler--Volmer equation force the surface concentrations to their
thermodynamic equilibrium values regardless of~$\alpha$, and the transfer
coefficient effectively drops out of the problem. In this regime, any
attempt to estimate~$\alpha$ from the voltammogram alone --- whether by
maximum likelihood or other point-estimation methods --- will yield an
essentially uninformative result.

\begin{figure}[ht]
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{figures/3-alpha-effect-reversible.png}
  \end{center}
  \caption{Effect of the transfer coefficient~$\alpha$ on the
    voltammogram in the reversible regime ($K_0 = 200$). The three
    curves are nearly indistinguishable, demonstrating that~$\alpha$
    has negligible influence on the voltammogram when the reaction
  kinetics are fast.}
  \label{fig:alpha-parameter-effect-rev}
\end{figure}

The combination of parameter correlations in the quasi-reversible regime,
the practical non-identifiability of~$\alpha$ in the reversible regime,
and the inherent noise present in experimental data means that estimating
the Butler--Volmer parameters is a problem better suited to a
probabilistic framework than to a purely optimisation-based approach. A
probabilistic treatment naturally quantifies the uncertainty in each
parameter and reveals correlations between them, providing a more
complete picture than a single point estimate. We develop this approach
in the following chapter.
