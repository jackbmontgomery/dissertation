\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:introduction}

Inverse problems -- recovering physical parameters from indirect
measurements -- arise throughout the sciences whenever the quantities of
interest cannot be observed directly. In electrochemistry, the
parameters governing a reaction at an electrode surface (rate constants,
transfer coefficients, formal potentials) are not measured but must be
inferred from the current response to an applied potential. This
inference is the electrochemical inverse problem, and it is the subject
of this dissertation.

The forward problem is well understood. In cyclic voltammetry, a
time-varying potential is applied to an electrode immersed in a solution
containing electroactive species, and the resulting current is recorded.
The current response is governed by two coupled processes: electron
transfer at the electrode surface, described by the Butler--Volmer
kinetic model \cite{dickinson2020butler}, and diffusion of species
through the solution, described by Fick's laws. Together these yield a
diffusion PDE whose solution predicts the current for a given set of
parameters \cite{compton2014understanding}. The inverse problem is to
invert this map. The difficulty is threefold: the mapping from parameters
to current is nonlinear, distinct parameter configurations can produce
similar voltammograms, and in certain kinetic regimes some parameters
cease to influence the current altogether.

Point estimates -- for instance, a least-squares fit -- cannot capture
these difficulties. They provide no account of uncertainty: they cannot
distinguish a well-constrained parameter from one to which the data are
insensitive, nor reveal correlations between parameters that may
compensate for one another. Bayesian inference addresses this by treating
the parameters as random variables and computing their posterior
distribution conditioned on the observed data
\cite{gavaghan2018bayesian}. The posterior encodes what the data can and
cannot tell us about the parameters: marginal widths quantify
uncertainty, off-diagonal structure reveals correlations, and
multimodality signals non-identifiability.

The standard tool for sampling such posteriors is Markov chain Monte
Carlo. In this setting each likelihood evaluation requires a PDE solve,
so every proposed sample -- accepted or rejected -- is expensive.
Gradient-free samplers such as random-walk Metropolis--Hastings (RWMH)
explore the parameter space by undirected local proposals and
consequently waste many of these solves on rejected candidates,
particularly as the number of parameters grows. Gradient information
helps in two ways: it can initialise the sampler near a posterior mode
via optimisation, reducing burn-in; and it can inform the sampling itself
through Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC), which uses the gradient to
construct long-range, low-rejection proposals.

The challenge is obtaining these gradients cheaply. Finite differences
require at least one additional forward solve per parameter and introduce
truncation error. Reverse-mode automatic differentiation computes exact
gradients at a cost bounded by a small multiple of the forward solve,
independent of parameter dimension
\cite{griewank2008evaluating}. Na\"ively taping an entire PDE solver,
however, records every intermediate operation -- including every step of
the linear system solve at each timestep -- incurring memory cost
proportional to the product of the number of timesteps and spatial grid
points. A more efficient approach is to derive a custom adjoint rule for
the banded linear solve and register it with the automatic
differentiation framework, so that gradient computation exploits the same
structure as the forward solve. This is the approach we adopt: the
adjoint of the tridiagonal (or pentadiagonal) solve is itself banded,
costing $O(n)$ per timestep with only the system matrix and solution
vector cached.

This work sits within a broader shift toward differentiable scientific
computing; Kitchin et~al.\ \cite{kitchin2025beyond} characterise
differentiable programming as a fifth paradigm in chemical engineering.
Concurrently, Chen et~al.\ \cite{chen2026differentiable} have developed
differentiable electrochemical simulators for gradient-based parameter
recovery. Our work is complementary: we adopt a Bayesian framework
rather than optimisation alone, and we compute gradients via a custom
adjoint rather than taping the full solver. The Bayesian approach to
voltammetric inference via MCMC was established by Gavaghan et~al.\
\cite{gavaghan2018bayesian}; we extend their framework by replacing the
gradient-free sampler with gradient-informed alternatives enabled by the
differentiable solver.

This dissertation makes three contributions. First, we develop a
differentiable forward solver for voltammetric PDEs, in which a custom
adjoint rule for the banded linear system provides exact gradients at low
memory cost. Second, we integrate this solver with the No-U-Turn sampler
(NUTS) \cite{hoffman2014no} to perform full Bayesian inference, obtaining
posterior distributions over the physical parameters. Third, we apply the
framework to three reaction mechanisms of increasing complexity -- a
single electron transfer (3~parameters), a heterogeneous reaction
(7~parameters), and an adsorption reaction (9~parameters) -- and show
that the advantage of NUTS over RWMH grows with the dimension of the
parameter space.

The methodology is developed through the single-electron-transfer
reaction, which serves as the running example through which each tool is
introduced and tested. Chapter~\ref{ch:electrochem} formulates the
forward problem as a diffusion PDE with Butler--Volmer boundary
conditions. Chapter~\ref{ch:numerical-methods} discretises this PDE and
verifies the solver, then examines parameter sensitivities and kinetic
regimes that motivate a probabilistic treatment.
Chapter~\ref{ch:bayesian} frames the inverse problem as Bayesian
inference and applies RWMH, demonstrating correct posterior recovery but
highlighting computational waste. Chapter~\ref{ch:adjoint} derives the
custom adjoint and demonstrates its value through gradient-based
optimisation. Chapter~\ref{ch:hmc} introduces HMC and NUTS, and
compares sampling efficiency against RWMH under equal computational
budgets. Chapters~\ref{ch:heterogeneous} and~\ref{ch:adsorption} then
extend the framework to the heterogeneous and adsorption reactions
without, showing that the approach scales to higher-dimensional
parameter spaces and chemically richer systems.
