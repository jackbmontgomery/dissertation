\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:introduction}

Electrochemistry is concerned with the interplay between electrical
potential and chemical change at the interface of an electrode and an
electrolyte solution. A central task in electrochemical research is to
characterise the kinetics and thermodynamics of redox reactions occurring
at the electrode surface. Voltammetry -- a family of techniques in which
the current through an electrochemical cell is recorded as the applied
potential is varied over time -- is among the most widely used methods for
this purpose \cite{compton2014understanding}.

The current response in a voltammetric experiment is governed by two
coupled processes: the rate of electron transfer at the electrode surface
and the transport of electroactive species through the solution. Under
the Butler--Volmer model \cite{dickinson2020butler} of electrode
kinetics, the electron transfer
rate depends on the applied potential through a formal
potential~$E_f^0$, a rate constant~$k_0$, and a transfer
coefficient~$\alpha$; the transport is described by Fick's laws of
diffusion. Together, these yield a diffusion partial differential
equation whose solution predicts the measured current for a given set of
parameters -- the \emph{forward problem}. We are concerned with the
\emph{inverse
problem}: given a measured, noisy current measurement, recover the physical
parameters that produced it. This is considerably harder. The mapping
from parameters to current is nonlinear, distinct parameter
configurations can produce similar voltammograms, and in certain kinetic
regimes some parameters cease to influence the current response
altogether.

A natural approach to the inverse problem is to seek the parameter
values that best fit the observed data, for instance by minimising a
least-squares objective. Such point estimates, however, provide no
principled account of the uncertainty in the inferred parameters: they
cannot distinguish a well-constrained parameter from one to which the
data are largely insensitive, nor can they reveal correlations between
parameters that may compensate for one another. Bayesian inference
addresses these limitations by treating the parameters as random
variables and seeking their posterior distribution conditioned on the
observed data \cite{gavaghan2018bayesian}. The posterior encodes
everything the data can and cannot tell us about the parameters, and its
structure -- marginal widths, correlations, multimodality -- directly
informs experimental design and scientific interpretation.

Markov chain Monte Carlo methods are the standard computational tool for
sampling from posterior distributions that lack closed-form expressions.
In the electrochemical setting, each evaluation of the likelihood
requires a numerical solution of the forward problem, so every proposed
sample -- whether accepted or rejected -- incurs the cost of a PDE solve.
Gradient-free samplers such as random-walk Metropolis--Hastings explore
the parameter space by local, undirected proposals and consequently
reject a large fraction of candidates, particularly as the number of
parameters grows. In this problem setting, where each wasted proposal is
expensive, the inefficiency of gradient-free sampling becomes a serious
computational bottleneck.

This motivates the use of gradient information. If the gradient of the
log-likelihood with respect to the parameters is available, it can be
exploited in two ways: first, to initialise the sampler near a mode of
the posterior via gradient-based optimisation, reducing the number of
samples discarded during burn-in; and second, to inform the sampling
itself through Hamiltonian Monte Carlo, which uses the gradient to
construct long-range, low-rejection proposals that explore the posterior
far more efficiently than undirected random walks.

The key computational challenge is obtaining these gradients cheaply.
Finite difference approximations require at least one additional forward
solve per parameter and suffer from truncation and cancellation errors.
Automatic differentiation avoids both issues by computing exact
derivatives at a cost that, in reverse mode, is bounded by a small
multiple of the forward solve regardless of the number of parameters
\cite{griewank2008evaluating}. Na\"ively applying reverse-mode
automatic differentiation to an entire PDE solver, however, requires
recording every intermediate operation -- including every step of the
linear system solve at each timestep -- resulting in substantial memory
overhead. A more efficient approach is to derive a custom adjoint rule
for the linear solve and supply it to the automatic differentiation
framework, so that the gradient computation exploits the same banded
structure as the forward solve. This is the strategy we adopt.

The idea of embedding differentiable simulation within scientific
inference is part of a broader shift in computational science. Kitchin
et~al.\ \cite{kitchin2025beyond} argue that differentiable programming
constitutes a fifth paradigm in chemical engineering, unifying
physics-based models with gradient-based learning and inference. The
present work is a concrete realisation of this vision in
electrochemistry: we make a PDE-based voltammetric simulator fully
differentiable and use the resulting gradients for rigorous Bayesian
inference.

Concurrently, Chen et~al.\ \cite{chen2026differentiable} have developed
end-to-end differentiable electrochemical simulators and demonstrated
gradient-based optimisation for parameter recovery across several
electrochemical systems. Our work is complementary in two respects.
First, we adopt a Bayesian framework rather than optimisation alone,
obtaining full posterior distributions with uncertainty quantification
rather than point estimates. Second, we compute gradients via a custom
adjoint of the banded linear solve rather than recording all operations
within the solver, yielding lower memory cost and better scaling to fine
discretisations. The Bayesian approach built on MCMC for voltammetric
inference was established by Gavaghan et~al.\
\cite{gavaghan2018bayesian}; we extend their framework by replacing the
gradient-free sampler with gradient-informed alternatives enabled by the
differentiable solver.

This dissertation makes three contributions of roughly equal weight.
First, we develop a differentiable forward solver for electrochemical
PDEs, in which a custom adjoint rule for the banded linear system
provides exact gradients at low cost. Second, we integrate this solver
with Hamiltonian Monte Carlo to perform full Bayesian inference,
obtaining posterior distributions that quantify parameter uncertainty and
reveal correlations. Third, we demonstrate that the framework extends
beyond the simple electron-transfer reaction to a heterogeneous reaction
with seven parameters and an adsorption reaction with nine, showing that
the advantage of gradient-based sampling grows with the dimension of the
parameter space.

The methodology is developed in the concrete setting of cyclic
voltammetry for a single-electron redox reaction, which serves as the
running example through which each tool is introduced and tested.
Chapter~\ref{ch:electrochem} formulates the forward problem as a
diffusion PDE with Butler--Volmer boundary conditions and
non-dimensionalises the system. Chapter~\ref{ch:numerical-methods}
discretises this PDE using an implicit finite difference scheme and
examines how the voltammogram depends on each parameter, exposing the
correlations and non-identifiability that motivate a probabilistic
treatment. Chapter~\ref{ch:bayesian} frames the inverse problem as
Bayesian inference and applies random-walk Metropolis--Hastings sampling,
demonstrating correct posterior recovery but highlighting the
computational cost of gradient-free proposals.
Chapter~\ref{ch:adjoint} develops the differentiable solver, introducing
reverse-mode automatic differentiation and deriving the custom adjoint
rule for the tridiagonal system. Chapter~\ref{ch:hmc} exploits these
gradients for Hamiltonian Monte Carlo sampling, showing improved
sampling efficiency over the gradient-free baseline. With the full
methodology established, Chapters~\ref{ch:heterogeneous}
and~\ref{ch:adsorption} extend the framework to a heterogeneous reaction
and an adsorption reaction respectively, demonstrating that the approach
scales to higher-dimensional parameter spaces and chemically richer
systems.
