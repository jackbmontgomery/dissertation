\chapter{Heterogeneous Reaction}\label{ch:heterogeneous}

This chapter applies the inference framework developed in
\cref{ch:bayesian,ch:adjoint,ch:hmc} to a reaction mechanism involving
two electron transfers coupled by an irreversible heterogeneous chemical
step. The parameter space grows from three to seven dimensions, providing
a first test of how the relative efficiency of NUTS and RWMH scales with
dimension. Because the methodology has already been established, we
focus on what is new: the reaction scheme, the coupled PDE system and its
boundary conditions, the reordering that yields a pentadiagonal linear
system, and the inference results. A more detailed treatment of
heterogeneous surface processes --- including explicit surface
concentrations and adsorption/desorption kinetics --- is deferred to
\cref{ch:adsorption}.

\section{Reaction Scheme}\label{sec:het-scheme}

We consider the $\mathrm{EC}_{\mathrm{irr,het}}\mathrm{E}$ mechanism
\cite{compton2014understanding}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:het-scheme}
  \begin{gathered}
    \mathrm{A} + \mathrm{e}^{-} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{B}, \\
    \mathrm{B} \xrightarrow{k_{\mathrm{het}}} \mathrm{C}, \\
    \mathrm{C} + \mathrm{e}^{-} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{D}.
  \end{gathered}
\end{equation}
Species~$\mathrm{A}$ is reduced to~$\mathrm{B}$ at the electrode
surface; $\mathrm{B}$ is then converted irreversibly to~$\mathrm{C}$ by
a surface-catalysed chemical process with rate constant
$k_{\mathrm{het}}$~($\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$); and $\mathrm{C}$ undergoes
a second electron transfer to form~$\mathrm{D}$. The chemical step
occurs only at the electrode surface, so the bulk transport of all four
species is governed by Fick's second law without additional kinetic
terms. This type of reaction appears, for example, in the
electroreduction of halonitroaromatic compounds in aprotic media
\cite{compton2014understanding}.

  $\mathrm{EC}_{\mathrm{irr,het}}\mathrm{E}$ reaction]{Schematic of
    the $\mathrm{EC}_{\mathrm{irr,het}}\mathrm{E}$ reaction mechanism.
    Two electrochemical steps (vertical) are coupled by an irreversible
  heterogeneous chemical step (horizontal) at the electrode surface.}

Each electron transfer is characterised by its own transfer
coefficient~$\alpha$, dimensionless rate constant~$K_0$, and formal
potential~$\theta_f$. Together with the dimensionless heterogeneous rate
constant~$K_{\mathrm{het}}$, the parameter vector is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:het-params}
  \phi = \bigl(\alpha^{(1)},\, K_0^{(1)},\, \theta_f^{(1)},\,
    \alpha^{(2)},\, K_0^{(2)},\, \theta_f^{(2)},\,
  K_{\mathrm{het}}\bigr) \in \mathbb{R}^7.
\end{equation}

\section{Mathematical Description}\label{sec:het-maths}

\subsection{Governing Equations}

All four species diffuse freely in solution. Under the equal-diffusion
assumption ($d_j = 1$ for all~$j$), each satisfies the same
dimensionless diffusion equation as in \cref{eq:nd_diffusion}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:het-diffusion}
  \frac{\partial C_j}{\partial T} = \frac{\partial^2 C_j}{\partial X^2},
  \qquad j \in \{\mathrm{A},\,\mathrm{B},\,\mathrm{C},\,\mathrm{D}\}.
\end{equation}
In the single-electron-transfer case of \cref{ch:electrochem}, the
equal-diffusion assumption reduced the system to a single PDE via the
conservation relation $C_\mathrm{A} + C_\mathrm{B} = \mathrm{const}$.
That reduction relied on stoichiometric conservation at the electrode:
every molecule of~$\mathrm{A}$ consumed produced exactly one molecule
of~$\mathrm{B}$, and vice versa. Here the heterogeneous step breaks
this balance --- $\mathrm{B}$ is consumed not only by oxidation back
to~$\mathrm{A}$ but also by conversion to~$\mathrm{C}$ --- so
$C_\mathrm{A} + C_\mathrm{B}$ is no longer conserved and all four
equations must be solved independently.

\subsection{Initial and Far-Field Conditions}

Initially only species~$\mathrm{A}$ is present in solution at its bulk
concentration:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:het-ic}
  C_\mathrm{A}(0, X) = 1, \qquad
  C_\mathrm{B}(0, X) = C_\mathrm{C}(0, X) = C_\mathrm{D}(0, X) = 0.
\end{equation}
These values are also imposed at the far-field boundary
$X = X_{\mathrm{max}}$.

\subsection{Electrode Boundary Conditions}

The boundary conditions at $X = 0$ couple the four species through the
Butler--Volmer kinetics of the two electron transfers and the
heterogeneous rate constant. In dimensional form
\cite{compton2014understanding}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:het-bc-dim}
  \begin{aligned}
    D_\mathrm{A}\left(\frac{\partial c_\mathrm{A}}{\partial x}\right)_{x=0}
    &= k_{\mathrm{red}}^{(1)}\, c_\mathrm{A}(0,t)
    - k_{\mathrm{ox}}^{(1)}\, c_\mathrm{B}(0,t), \\
    D_\mathrm{B}\left(\frac{\partial c_\mathrm{B}}{\partial x}\right)_{x=0}
    &= -k_{\mathrm{red}}^{(1)}\, c_\mathrm{A}(0,t)
    + k_{\mathrm{ox}}^{(1)}\, c_\mathrm{B}(0,t)
    + k_{\mathrm{het}}\, c_\mathrm{B}(0,t), \\
    D_\mathrm{C}\left(\frac{\partial c_\mathrm{C}}{\partial x}\right)_{x=0}
    &= k_{\mathrm{red}}^{(2)}\, c_\mathrm{C}(0,t)
    - k_{\mathrm{ox}}^{(2)}\, c_\mathrm{D}(0,t)
    - k_{\mathrm{het}}\, c_\mathrm{B}(0,t), \\
    D_\mathrm{D}\left(\frac{\partial c_\mathrm{D}}{\partial x}\right)_{x=0}
    &= -k_{\mathrm{red}}^{(2)}\, c_\mathrm{C}(0,t)
    + k_{\mathrm{ox}}^{(2)}\, c_\mathrm{D}(0,t).
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The first and fourth rows have the same structure as the
single-electron-transfer boundary condition of \cref{ch:electrochem}:
they balance the diffusive flux against the net electrochemical rate. The
second and third rows contain the additional $k_{\mathrm{het}}\,
c_\mathrm{B}(0,t)$ term: positive in the $\mathrm{B}$~equation
(consuming~$\mathrm{B}$) and negative in the $\mathrm{C}$~equation
(producing~$\mathrm{C}$). This term is the sole coupling between the
two electron-transfer subsystems.

\subsection{Non-Dimensionalisation}

We apply the same scalings as in \cref{tab:normalisation}, defining
dimensionless rate constants for each electron transfer via
\begin{equation}\label{eq:het-kred-kox}
  K_{\mathrm{red}}^{(i)} = K_0^{(i)}
  \exp\!\bigl[-\alpha^{(i)}\bigl(\theta(T) - \theta_f^{(i)}\bigr)\bigr],
  \qquad
  K_{\mathrm{ox}}^{(i)} = K_0^{(i)}
  \exp\!\bigl[(1-\alpha^{(i)})\bigl(\theta(T) - \theta_f^{(i)}\bigr)\bigr],
\end{equation}
for $i = 1, 2$, generalising \cref{eq:kred_kox}. The dimensionless
heterogeneous rate constant is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Khet}
  K_{\mathrm{het}} = \frac{k_{\mathrm{het}}\,\epsilon}{D_\mathrm{A}}.
\end{equation}
Under the equal-diffusion assumption ($d_j = 1$), the boundary
conditions~\cref{eq:het-bc-dim} become
\begin{equation}\label{eq:het-bc-nondim}
  \begin{aligned}
    \left(\frac{\partial C_\mathrm{A}}{\partial X}\right)_{X=0}
    &= K_{\mathrm{red}}^{(1)}\, C_\mathrm{A}(0,T)
    - K_{\mathrm{ox}}^{(1)}\, C_\mathrm{B}(0,T), \\[4pt]
    \left(\frac{\partial C_\mathrm{B}}{\partial X}\right)_{X=0}
    &= -K_{\mathrm{red}}^{(1)}\, C_\mathrm{A}(0,T)
    + \bigl(K_{\mathrm{ox}}^{(1)} + K_{\mathrm{het}}\bigr)\,
    C_\mathrm{B}(0,T), \\[4pt]
    \left(\frac{\partial C_\mathrm{C}}{\partial X}\right)_{X=0}
    &= K_{\mathrm{red}}^{(2)}\, C_\mathrm{C}(0,T)
    - K_{\mathrm{ox}}^{(2)}\, C_\mathrm{D}(0,T)
    - K_{\mathrm{het}}\, C_\mathrm{B}(0,T), \\[4pt]
    \left(\frac{\partial C_\mathrm{D}}{\partial X}\right)_{X=0}
    &= -K_{\mathrm{red}}^{(2)}\, C_\mathrm{C}(0,T)
    + K_{\mathrm{ox}}^{(2)}\, C_\mathrm{D}(0,T).
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The $K_{\mathrm{het}}$ term appears in the boundary conditions for
$\mathrm{B}$ and $\mathrm{C}$ and nowhere else; the bulk equations and
far-field conditions are identical to those of the single-species case.

\subsection{Current}

The measured current is the sum of the fluxes from both electrochemical
steps plus the contribution from the heterogeneous conversion of
$\mathrm{B}$ at the surface:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:het-current}
  \frac{I}{FA} = -\left\{
    D_\mathrm{A}\left(\frac{\partial c_\mathrm{A}}{\partial x}\right)_{x=0}
    + D_\mathrm{C}\left(\frac{\partial c_\mathrm{C}}{\partial x}\right)_{x=0}
    + k_{\mathrm{het}}\, c_\mathrm{B}(0,t)
  \right\}.
\end{equation}
The $k_{\mathrm{het}}\, c_\mathrm{B}$ term accounts for the electrons
transferred indirectly: each molecule of~$\mathrm{B}$ converted
to~$\mathrm{C}$ at the surface is equivalent to one electron passing
through the external circuit, because $\mathrm{C}$ can subsequently be
reduced to~$\mathrm{D}$.

Each species is discretised on the same exponentially expanding spatial
mesh described in \cref{sec:discretisation}. At each timestep the four
species are coupled only through the boundary conditions at $X = 0$; in
the interior each satisfies an independent tridiagonal system. A natural
ordering of the unknowns as
$(C_\mathrm{A}^0, \ldots, C_\mathrm{A}^{n-1},\,
  C_\mathrm{B}^0, \ldots, C_\mathrm{B}^{n-1},\,
C_\mathrm{C}^0, \ldots, C_\mathrm{D}^{n-1})$
produces a block-tridiagonal system in which the $K_{\mathrm{het}}$
coupling between $\mathrm{B}$ and $\mathrm{C}$ creates off-diagonal
entries separated by $n$ positions from the main diagonal --- far outside
any narrow band.

The bandwidth can be reduced by reordering the unknowns. We index
$\mathrm{A}$ and $\mathrm{B}$ from $X_{n-1}$ down to $X_0$ (far field
to electrode) and $\mathrm{C}$ and $\mathrm{D}$ from $X_0$ up to
$X_{n-1}$ (electrode to far field), interleaving the four species so that
the electrode-surface nodes of all species are adjacent. Concretely, the
unknown vector is ordered as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:het-ordering}
  \mathbf{C} = \bigl(
    C_\mathrm{A}^{n-1}, C_\mathrm{B}^{n-1}, \ldots,
    C_\mathrm{A}^{0}, C_\mathrm{B}^{0},\,
    C_\mathrm{C}^{0}, C_\mathrm{D}^{0}, \ldots,
    C_\mathrm{C}^{n-1}, C_\mathrm{D}^{n-1}
  \bigr)^T.
\end{equation}
In this ordering the interior stencil for each species occupies three
diagonals (stride~2 apart, since the two species at each spatial node are
interleaved). The $K_{\mathrm{het}}$ coupling between $C_\mathrm{B}^0$
and $C_\mathrm{C}^0$ now falls within two positions of the main diagonal.
The result is a pentadiagonal system that can be solved in $O(n)$
operations per timestep using a banded LU factorisation.

As noted in \cref{sec:pentadiagonal}, the transpose of a pentadiagonal
matrix is again pentadiagonal, so the adjoint system required for gradient
computation is solved at the same $O(n)$ cost. The custom VJP rule
generalises directly from the tridiagonal case.

\section{Inference Setup}\label{sec:het-inference}

The inference workflow follows \cref{sec:workflow} without modification.
The seven parameters are transformed to unconstrained space:
$\alpha^{(i)} \mapsto \operatorname{logit}(\alpha^{(i)})$,
$K_0^{(i)} \mapsto \log K_0^{(i)}$, and
$K_{\mathrm{het}} \mapsto \log K_{\mathrm{het}}$,
with $\theta_f^{(i)}$ left untransformed. Uniform priors
are placed on the transformed parameters over a range chosen to be wide
relative to the posterior.

Synthetic data are generated at the true parameter values
$\phi^* = (0.5,\, 15.0,\, 0.2,\, 0.5,\, 6.0,\, 0.4,\, 25.0)$,
with dimensionless scan rate $\sigma = 1000$ and noise level
$\eta = 0.02$ (the same values used in \cref{ch:hmc}).

\section{Results}\label{sec:het-results}

\subsection{Solver Verification}\label{sec:het-verification}

We verify the forward solver by checking two limiting cases
\cite{prieto1997electrode}. When $K_{\mathrm{het}} \to 0$ the chemical
step is inactive: no $\mathrm{C}$ or $\mathrm{D}$ is produced, and the
system reduces to a single electron transfer
$\mathrm{A} + \mathrm{e}^- \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{B}$. The peak
current and peak potential should therefore match the irreversible
single-electron expressions of \cref{eq:irreversible-peak}. In the
opposite limit, $K_{\mathrm{het}} \to \infty$ with $K_0^{(2)} \to
\infty$, every molecule of~$\mathrm{B}$ produced at the surface is
instantly converted to~$\mathrm{C}$ and immediately reduced
to~$\mathrm{D}$. The reaction then behaves as a single two-electron
transfer, and the peak current approaches twice the single-electron
value. \Cref{fig:het-analytical} confirms that the numerical solver
reproduces both limits: the dashed lines indicate the analytical peak
current and peak potential in each case.

\subsection{Posterior Distributions}\label{sec:het-posteriors}

\Cref{fig:het-corner} shows selected pairwise marginal posteriors
obtained by NUTS and RWMH under equal computational budgets. Both
samplers recover the true parameter values across all seven dimensions,
with the marginals concentrating around the dashed lines. The NUTS and
RWMH contours agree, confirming convergence to the same target
distribution ($\hat{R} < 1.01$ for all parameters). The formal potentials
$\theta_f^{(1)}$ and $\theta_f^{(2)}$ are omitted from this plot for
clarity; their marginals are similarly well-recovered.

The off-diagonal panels reveal the correlation structure of the
posterior. There is visible correlation between $\alpha^{(1)}$ and
$K_0^{(1)}$, and between $\alpha^{(2)}$ and $K_0^{(2)}$, reflecting
the same kinetic trade-off observed in the single-electron case
(\cref{sec:bv-effects}): increasing $\alpha$ while decreasing $K_0$ can
produce a similar voltammogram. The RWMH contours are noticeably broader
than those of NUTS; this is not a difference in the target distribution
but a consequence of higher autocorrelation in the RWMH chain, which
produces fewer independent samples within the same budget.

\subsection{Sampling Efficiency}\label{sec:het-ess}

\Cref{fig:het-ess} compares the effective sample size (ESS) of NUTS and
RWMH as a function of the sampling budget, measured in forward-solve
equivalents. For every parameter, NUTS accumulates independent samples at
a higher rate than RWMH. The gap is consistent across all seven
parameters, with NUTS achieving roughly three to four times the ESS of
RWMH at the end of the run. Compared with the three-parameter
electron-transfer case (\cref{sec:hmc-results}), the relative advantage
of NUTS has grown: in the lower-dimensional problem the ESS ratio was
approximately two, whereas here it is three to four. This is consistent
with the known scaling behaviour of random-walk proposals, whose mixing
time grows with dimension, while NUTS proposals exploit gradient
information to maintain efficient exploration.

\section{AC Voltammetry}\label{sec:het-ac}

The DC voltammograms studied so far use a triangular potential waveform.
In AC voltammetry, a sinusoidal perturbation is superimposed on the DC
ramp, so that the applied dimensionless potential becomes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ac-potential}
  \theta(T) = \theta_{\mathrm{DC}}(T) + \Delta\theta\,
  \sin(2\pi f\, T),
\end{equation}
where $\Delta\theta$ is the amplitude and $f$ is the frequency of the
perturbation. The resulting current contains both a slowly varying DC
component and oscillatory contributions at harmonics of~$f$.

The motivation for considering AC voltammetry is that the oscillatory
component of the current probes the electrode kinetics on a faster
timescale than the DC sweep alone, providing additional information about
the rate constants and transfer coefficients. Gavaghan
et~al.~\cite{gavaghan2018bayesian} demonstrated this for the
single-electron-transfer reaction: the posteriors obtained from AC data
were narrower than those from DC data at the same noise level. We repeat
this comparison for the heterogeneous reaction.

\Cref{fig:ac-voltam} shows the AC voltammogram for the heterogeneous
reaction at the true parameter values. The sinusoidal perturbation
produces oscillations superimposed on the DC envelope; the oscillations
are largest near the formal potentials, where the Butler--Volmer kinetics
are most sensitive to potential changes. No changes are required to the
forward solver or the inference workflow: the only modification is the
potential waveform~\cref{eq:ac-potential}, which enters through the
time-dependent $K_{\mathrm{red}}^{(i)}$ and $K_{\mathrm{ox}}^{(i)}$
terms in the boundary conditions.

\Cref{fig:ac-dc-comparison} overlays the marginal posteriors obtained
from DC and AC data using NUTS. For every parameter the AC posteriors
are narrower than, or comparable to, the DC posteriors, with both
centred on the true values. The effect is most pronounced for
$K_0^{(1)}$ and $\theta_f^{(1)}$, where the AC marginals are
noticeably tighter. This is consistent with the observation of Gavaghan
et~al.~\cite{gavaghan2018bayesian}: the oscillatory component of the AC
current carries additional information about the electrode kinetics that
is absent from the DC waveform. The posterior for $K_{\mathrm{het}}$ also
narrows under AC, indicating that the sinusoidal perturbation helps
constrain the heterogeneous rate constant.
