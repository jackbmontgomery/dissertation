\chapter{Adsorption Reaction}\label{ch:adsorption}

This chapter applies the inference framework to a reaction involving
adsorption of the electroactive species onto the electrode surface. The
adsorption process introduces surface coverage variables governed by
ordinary differential equations coupled to the diffusion PDE through the
boundary conditions. The Langmuir adsorption model leads to nonlinear
terms --- products of surface coverages and surface concentrations ---
that prevent a direct banded linear solve. We describe three
discretisation strategies with different trade-offs between accuracy and
compatibility with automatic differentiation, and select the one that
permits gradient computation through JAX while reproducing the current
response with sufficient fidelity. The parameter space grows to nine
dimensions, completing the dimensionality sequence $3 \to 7 \to 9$ that
has been the recurring comparison between NUTS and RWMH.

\section{Reaction Scheme}\label{sec:ads-scheme}

We consider a single electron transfer in which both the reactant
$\mathrm{A}$ and the product $\mathrm{B}$ can adsorb on the electrode
surface \cite{compton2014understanding}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ads-scheme}
  \mathrm{A} + \mathrm{e}^{-} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{B}.
\end{equation}
The electron transfer can proceed through both the solution-phase
species (with rate constants $k_{\mathrm{red/ox}}^{\mathrm{sol}}$) and
the surface-bound species (with rate constants
$k_{\mathrm{red/ox}}^{\mathrm{ads}}$). In addition, each species
adsorbs onto and desorbs from the electrode surface with its own rate
constants $k_{\mathrm{ads}}^{j}$ and $k_{\mathrm{des}}^{j}$, competing
for a finite number of surface sites characterised by the maximum
surface concentration $\Gamma_{\mathrm{max}}$
(\cref{fig:ads-schematic}).

\begin{figure}
  \centering
  \input{figures/adsorption-reaction.tex}
  \caption[Schematic of the adsorption reaction]{Schematic of the
    adsorption reaction mechanism. Species $\mathrm{A}$ and $\mathrm{B}$
    exist in both a solution phase and a surface-bound (adsorbed) phase.
    Electron transfer can occur between both solution-phase and
    adsorbed species. Adsorption and desorption exchange species between
  the two phases.}
  \label{fig:ads-schematic}
\end{figure}

The solution-phase and adsorbed electron transfers are each
characterised by their own transfer coefficient and rate constant:
$(\alpha^{\mathrm{sol}},\, K_0^{\mathrm{sol}})$ and
$(\alpha^{\mathrm{ads}},\, K_0^{\mathrm{ads}})$ respectively. The nine
parameters to be inferred are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ads-params}
  \phi = \bigl(\alpha^{\mathrm{sol}},\, K_0^{\mathrm{sol}},\,
    \theta_f^{\mathrm{sol}},\,
    \alpha^{\mathrm{ads}},\, K_0^{\mathrm{ads}},\,
    K_{\mathrm{ads}}^{\mathrm{A}},\, K_{\mathrm{des}}^{\mathrm{A}},\,
    K_{\mathrm{ads}}^{\mathrm{B}},\, K_{\mathrm{des}}^{\mathrm{B}}
  \bigr) \in \mathbb{R}^9.
\end{equation}
The saturation parameter $\beta = c_\mathrm{A}^*\,\epsilon /
\Gamma_{\mathrm{max}}$ is treated as known and fixed at $\beta = 1$; in
practice it is determined by the bulk concentration and electrode
geometry.

\section{Mathematical Description}\label{sec:ads-maths}

\subsection{Governing Equations}

The transport of $\mathrm{A}$ and $\mathrm{B}$ in solution is governed
by the same dimensionless diffusion equation as in
\cref{eq:nd_diffusion}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ads-diffusion}
  \frac{\partial C_j}{\partial T} = \frac{\partial^2 C_j}{\partial X^2},
  \qquad j \in \{\mathrm{A},\,\mathrm{B}\}.
\end{equation}
Unlike the heterogeneous reaction of \cref{ch:heterogeneous}, there are
no additional kinetic source terms in the bulk. All coupling between the
species enters through the boundary conditions at the electrode surface.

\subsection{Surface Coverage Equations}

The amounts of $\mathrm{A}$ and $\mathrm{B}$ adsorbed on the electrode
surface are described by the surface coverages $\xi_j =
\Gamma_j / \Gamma_{\mathrm{max}}$, where $\Gamma_j$
($\mathrm{mol\,m^{-2}}$) is the surface concentration of species~$j$.
These evolve according to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ads-coverage-ode}
  \begin{aligned}
    \frac{\mathrm{d}\xi_\mathrm{A}}{\mathrm{d}T}
    &= -K_{\mathrm{red}}^{\mathrm{ads}}\,\xi_\mathrm{A}
    + K_{\mathrm{ox}}^{\mathrm{ads}}\,\xi_\mathrm{B}
    + K_{\mathrm{ads}}^{\mathrm{A}}\,C_{0,\mathrm{A}}
    \bigl[1 - (\xi_\mathrm{A} + \xi_\mathrm{B})\bigr]
    - K_{\mathrm{des}}^{\mathrm{A}}\,\xi_\mathrm{A}, \\[4pt]
    \frac{\mathrm{d}\xi_\mathrm{B}}{\mathrm{d}T}
    &= K_{\mathrm{red}}^{\mathrm{ads}}\,\xi_\mathrm{A}
    - K_{\mathrm{ox}}^{\mathrm{ads}}\,\xi_\mathrm{B}
    + K_{\mathrm{ads}}^{\mathrm{B}}\,C_{0,\mathrm{B}}
    \bigl[1 - (\xi_\mathrm{A} + \xi_\mathrm{B})\bigr]
    - K_{\mathrm{des}}^{\mathrm{B}}\,\xi_\mathrm{B},
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $C_{0,j} = C_j(T, 0)$ is the surface concentration in solution
and we have set $\beta = 1$.
The first two terms in each equation are the electron transfer between
adsorbed species; the third is adsorption according to the Langmuir
isotherm, with the factor $[1 - (\xi_\mathrm{A} + \xi_\mathrm{B})]$
representing the fraction of unoccupied surface sites; the fourth is
desorption.

\subsection{Electrode Boundary Conditions}

The surface flux of each solution-phase species is determined by both
the electron transfer in solution and the net adsorption/desorption
flux:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ads-bc}
  \begin{aligned}
    \left(\frac{\partial C_\mathrm{A}}{\partial X}\right)_{X=0}
    &= K_{\mathrm{red}}^{\mathrm{sol}}\,C_{0,\mathrm{A}}
    - K_{\mathrm{ox}}^{\mathrm{sol}}\,C_{0,\mathrm{B}}
    + K_{\mathrm{ads}}^{\mathrm{A}}\,C_{0,\mathrm{A}}
    \bigl[1 - (\xi_\mathrm{A} + \xi_\mathrm{B})\bigr]
    - K_{\mathrm{des}}^{\mathrm{A}}\,\xi_\mathrm{A}, \\[4pt]
    \left(\frac{\partial C_\mathrm{B}}{\partial X}\right)_{X=0}
    &= \frac{1}{d_\mathrm{B}}\Bigl\{
      -K_{\mathrm{red}}^{\mathrm{sol}}\,C_{0,\mathrm{A}}
      + K_{\mathrm{ox}}^{\mathrm{sol}}\,C_{0,\mathrm{B}}
      + K_{\mathrm{ads}}^{\mathrm{B}}\,C_{0,\mathrm{B}}
      \bigl[1 - (\xi_\mathrm{A} + \xi_\mathrm{B})\bigr]
      - K_{\mathrm{des}}^{\mathrm{B}}\,\xi_\mathrm{B}
    \Bigr\}.
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The dimensionless rate constants for the solution-phase and adsorbed
electron transfers follow the Butler--Volmer form with their respective
transfer coefficients:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ads-kred-kox}
  \begin{aligned}
    K_{\mathrm{red}}^{\mathrm{sol}}
    &= K_0^{\mathrm{sol}}
    \exp\!\bigl[-\alpha^{\mathrm{sol}}\bigl(\theta(T) -
    \theta_f^{\mathrm{sol}}\bigr)\bigr],
    &\qquad
    K_{\mathrm{ox}}^{\mathrm{sol}}
    &= K_0^{\mathrm{sol}}
    \exp\!\bigl[(1-\alpha^{\mathrm{sol}})\bigl(\theta(T) -
    \theta_f^{\mathrm{sol}}\bigr)\bigr],
    \\[4pt]
    K_{\mathrm{red}}^{\mathrm{ads}}
    &= K_0^{\mathrm{ads}}
    \exp\!\bigl[-\alpha^{\mathrm{ads}}\bigl(\theta(T) -
    \theta_f^{\mathrm{ads}}\bigr)\bigr],
    &\qquad
    K_{\mathrm{ox}}^{\mathrm{ads}}
    &= K_0^{\mathrm{ads}}
    \exp\!\bigl[(1-\alpha^{\mathrm{ads}})\bigl(\theta(T) -
    \theta_f^{\mathrm{ads}}\bigr)\bigr],
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $K_0^{\mathrm{sol}} = k_0^{\mathrm{sol}}\,\epsilon / D_\mathrm{A}$
and $K_0^{\mathrm{ads}} = k_0^{\mathrm{ads}}\,\epsilon^2 / D_\mathrm{A}$
are the dimensionless standard rate constants. The solution-phase and
adsorbed couples share the same underlying redox process, so their
formal potentials are related through the adsorption equilibrium
constants \cite{compton2014understanding}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ads-thetaf-relation}
  \theta_f^{\mathrm{ads}} = \theta_f^{\mathrm{sol}}
  - \ln\!\left(
    \frac{K_{\mathrm{ads}}^{\mathrm{A}} / K_{\mathrm{des}}^{\mathrm{A}}}
    {K_{\mathrm{ads}}^{\mathrm{B}} / K_{\mathrm{des}}^{\mathrm{B}}}
  \right).
\end{equation}
This is not an additional free parameter: $\theta_f^{\mathrm{ads}}$ is
determined by $\theta_f^{\mathrm{sol}}$ and the four
adsorption/desorption rate constants already in~$\phi$.
The adsorption/desorption rate constants are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ads-kads-kdes}
  K_{\mathrm{ads}}^{j}
  = \frac{k_{\mathrm{ads}}^{j}\,\Gamma_{\mathrm{max}}\,\epsilon}
  {D_\mathrm{A}},
  \qquad
  K_{\mathrm{des}}^{j}
  = \frac{k_{\mathrm{des}}^{j}\,\Gamma_{\mathrm{max}}\,\epsilon}
  {c_\mathrm{A}^*\,D_\mathrm{A}}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Initial Conditions}

Initially only species $\mathrm{A}$ is present in solution at its bulk
concentration:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ads-ic-conc}
  C_\mathrm{A}(0, X) = 1, \qquad C_\mathrm{B}(0, X) = 0.
\end{equation}
The initial surface coverages are determined by the Langmuir equilibrium
with the bulk solution:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ads-ic-coverage}
  \xi_\mathrm{A}(0) =
  \frac{K_{\mathrm{ads}}^{\mathrm{A}} / K_{\mathrm{des}}^{\mathrm{A}}}
  {1 + K_{\mathrm{ads}}^{\mathrm{A}} / K_{\mathrm{des}}^{\mathrm{A}}
    + C_\mathrm{B}^*\, K_{\mathrm{ads}}^{\mathrm{B}} /
  K_{\mathrm{des}}^{\mathrm{B}}},
  \qquad
  \xi_\mathrm{B}(0) =
  \frac{C_\mathrm{B}^*\, K_{\mathrm{ads}}^{\mathrm{B}} /
  K_{\mathrm{des}}^{\mathrm{B}}}
  {1 + K_{\mathrm{ads}}^{\mathrm{A}} / K_{\mathrm{des}}^{\mathrm{A}}
    + C_\mathrm{B}^*\, K_{\mathrm{ads}}^{\mathrm{B}} /
  K_{\mathrm{des}}^{\mathrm{B}}}.
\end{equation}
With $C_\mathrm{B}^* = 0$ this simplifies to
$\xi_\mathrm{B}(0) = 0$ and
$\xi_\mathrm{A}(0) = (K_{\mathrm{ads}}^{\mathrm{A}} /
K_{\mathrm{des}}^{\mathrm{A}}) / (1 +
K_{\mathrm{ads}}^{\mathrm{A}} / K_{\mathrm{des}}^{\mathrm{A}})$.

\subsection{Current}

The measured current has contributions from both the solution-phase and
adsorbed electron transfers:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ads-current}
  \frac{I}{\pi\epsilon F D_\mathrm{A} c_\mathrm{A}^*}
  = -\left\{
    \left(\frac{\partial C_\mathrm{A}}{\partial X}\right)_{X=0}
    - \frac{1}{\beta}
    \frac{\mathrm{d}\xi_\mathrm{A}}{\mathrm{d}T}
  \right\}.
\end{equation}
The first term is the diffusive flux from the solution-phase reaction;
the second accounts for the charge transferred by the conversion of
adsorbed $\mathrm{A}$ to adsorbed $\mathrm{B}$.

\section{Nonlinearity and Discretisation}\label{sec:ads-disc}

\subsection{Source of Nonlinearity}

The Langmuir adsorption terms in \cref{eq:ads-coverage-ode,eq:ads-bc}
contain products of the unknown surface concentrations $C_{0,j}$ and
the unknown surface coverages $\xi_j$, specifically terms of the form
$C_{0,j}\,[1 - (\xi_\mathrm{A} + \xi_\mathrm{B})]$. After
discretisation with the backward Euler scheme these become products of
unknowns at the current timestep~$k$, making the algebraic system
nonlinear. This is in contrast to the heterogeneous reaction of
\cref{ch:heterogeneous}, where all coupling terms were linear in the
unknowns and the system could be solved directly as a banded linear
system at each timestep.

\subsection{Newton--Raphson Solver}\label{sec:ads-newton}

We order the unknowns at each timestep as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ads-ordering}
  \mathbf{x} = \bigl(
    \xi_\mathrm{A}^k,\, \xi_\mathrm{B}^k,\,
    C_{0,\mathrm{A}}^k,\, C_{0,\mathrm{B}}^k,\,
    C_{1,\mathrm{A}}^k,\, C_{1,\mathrm{B}}^k,\,
    \ldots,\,
    C_{n-1,\mathrm{A}}^k,\, C_{n-1,\mathrm{B}}^k
  \bigr)^T \in \mathbb{R}^{2n+2},
\end{equation}
and define the residual vector $\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}) = 0$, where each
component is one of the discretised equations. The first two components
are the backward-Euler discretisation of
\cref{eq:ads-coverage-ode}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ads-disc-coverage}
  \begin{aligned}
    f_0 &=
    \frac{\xi_\mathrm{A}^k - \xi_\mathrm{A}^{k-1}}{\Delta T}
    + K_{\mathrm{red}}^{\mathrm{ads}}\,\xi_\mathrm{A}^k
    - K_{\mathrm{ox}}^{\mathrm{ads}}\,\xi_\mathrm{B}^k
    - K_{\mathrm{ads}}^{\mathrm{A}}\,\beta\,C_{0,\mathrm{A}}^k
    \bigl[1 - (\xi_\mathrm{A}^k + \xi_\mathrm{B}^k)\bigr]
    + K_{\mathrm{des}}^{\mathrm{A}}\,\beta\,\xi_\mathrm{A}^k, \\[4pt]
    f_1 &=
    \frac{\xi_\mathrm{B}^k - \xi_\mathrm{B}^{k-1}}{\Delta T}
    - K_{\mathrm{red}}^{\mathrm{ads}}\,\xi_\mathrm{A}^k
    + K_{\mathrm{ox}}^{\mathrm{ads}}\,\xi_\mathrm{B}^k
    - K_{\mathrm{ads}}^{\mathrm{B}}\,\beta\,C_{0,\mathrm{B}}^k
    \bigl[1 - (\xi_\mathrm{A}^k + \xi_\mathrm{B}^k)\bigr]
    + K_{\mathrm{des}}^{\mathrm{B}}\,\beta\,\xi_\mathrm{B}^k.
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The next two components are the discretised boundary
conditions~\cref{eq:ads-bc}, and the remaining $2(n-1)$ components are
the standard backward-Euler diffusion equations for each species at the
interior and far-field nodes.

The Jacobian $\mathbf{J} = \partial\mathbf{F}/\partial\mathbf{x}$
has a sparse structure. The first four rows (corresponding to
$f_0,\ldots,f_3$) are coupled to the surface coverage and surface
concentration unknowns. After a preliminary row elimination to remove
the $\xi_\mathrm{A}^k$ dependence from~$f_3$
\cite{compton2014understanding}, the remaining system reduces to a
pentadiagonal form that can be solved in $O(n)$ operations. The
Newton--Raphson iteration is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ads-newton}
  \mathbf{J}(\mathbf{x}_s)\,\delta\mathbf{x}
  = -\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}_s),
  \qquad
  \mathbf{x}_{s+1} = \mathbf{x}_s + \delta\mathbf{x},
\end{equation}
and is repeated until $\|\delta\mathbf{x}\|_\infty < \varepsilon$. The
initial guess at each timestep is the solution from the previous
timestep, which provides a good starting point since the potential
changes smoothly between steps. In practice two to four iterations
suffice.

This solver serves as the ground-truth reference. However, the
Newton iteration involves a variable number of iterations controlled by
a convergence check --- a \texttt{while} loop whose iteration count
depends on the input values. JAX requires fixed control flow for
tracing: a \texttt{while} loop with a data-dependent termination
condition cannot be traced through, so the Newton solver cannot be
differentiated by the standard adjoint machinery of
\cref{ch:adjoint}. We therefore require an alternative solver for
inference.

\subsection{Alternative Solvers}\label{sec:ads-alt-solvers}

Two alternative discretisation strategies avoid the Newton iteration by
linearising the nonlinear terms at each timestep, producing a linear
system that can be solved directly and differentiated through.

\paragraph{Explicit linearisation.}
The nonlinear products $C_{0,j}^k\,[1 - (\xi_\mathrm{A}^k +
\xi_\mathrm{B}^k)]$ are approximated by evaluating the surface
coverage at the previous timestep:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ads-explicit}
  C_{0,j}^k\,\bigl[1 - (\xi_\mathrm{A}^k + \xi_\mathrm{B}^k)\bigr]
  \approx
  C_{0,j}^k\,\bigl[1 - (\xi_\mathrm{A}^{k-1} + \xi_\mathrm{B}^{k-1})\bigr].
\end{equation}
This replaces the product of two unknowns at timestep~$k$ with a
product of one unknown ($C_{0,j}^k$) and a known quantity from
timestep~$k-1$. The resulting system is linear in the unknowns at
each timestep and can be assembled into a banded matrix and solved
directly. The approximation is first-order in $\Delta T$ and is
accurate when the surface coverage does not change rapidly between
timesteps.

\paragraph{Backward implicit linearisation.}
Following Britz~\cite{britz2005digital}, the product of two unknowns
at timestep~$k$ is approximated as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ads-britz}
  C_{0,j}^k\,\xi_l^k
  \approx
  C_{0,j}^k\,\xi_l^{k-1}
  + C_{0,j}^{k-1}\,\xi_l^k
  - C_{0,j}^{k-1}\,\xi_l^{k-1}.
\end{equation}
This is the same linearisation used for second-order catalytic
mechanisms \cite{compton2014understanding}: the product is expanded to
first order about the previous-timestep values, retaining linear terms
in both unknowns. The resulting system is again linear at each timestep
and admits a banded solve. This approximation is also first-order in
$\Delta T$ but retains more implicit coupling than the explicit
scheme.

Both alternatives produce a linear system at each timestep with the
same pentadiagonal structure as the Newton solver (after the
preliminary row elimination), and both are compatible with the adjoint
differentiation framework of \cref{ch:adjoint}.

\subsection{Solver Comparison}\label{sec:ads-solver-comparison}

\Cref{fig:ads-solver-comparison} compares the two AD-compatible solvers
against the Newton reference by plotting the absolute relative error in
the current at each timestep on a logarithmic scale. The backward
implicit scheme is three to four orders of magnitude more accurate than
the explicit scheme across most of the potential sweep. The difference
is largest near the current peaks, where the surface coverage changes
rapidly between timesteps and the explicit approximation --- which
evaluates $\xi_j$ at the previous timestep --- lags behind the true
solution. The backward implicit linearisation retains linear coupling to
$\xi_j^k$ at the current timestep, tracking these rapid changes more
faithfully.

\begin{figure}[ht]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/8-solver-comparison.png}
  \caption[Solver comparison for the adsorption reaction]{Absolute
    relative error of the explicit (blue) and backward implicit (orange)
    solvers against the Newton reference, evaluated at each timestep.

  \label{fig:ads-solver-comparison}

The backward implicit solver is slower than the explicit solver (80\,ms
vs 60\,ms per forward solve) due to the additional terms in the matrix
assembly from the linearisation~\cref{eq:ads-britz}. This 33\%
overhead is acceptable given the accuracy gain, and we adopt the
backward implicit scheme for all inference runs. The Newton solver is
retained as the ground-truth reference for verification.

\section{Inference Setup}\label{sec:ads-inference}

The inference workflow follows \cref{sec:workflow} without modification.
The nine parameters are transformed to unconstrained space:
$\alpha^{\mathrm{sol}} \mapsto \operatorname{logit}(\alpha^{\mathrm{sol}})$,
$\alpha^{\mathrm{ads}} \mapsto \operatorname{logit}(\alpha^{\mathrm{ads}})$,
and $K_0^{\mathrm{sol}}$, $K_0^{\mathrm{ads}}$,
$K_{\mathrm{ads}}^{\mathrm{A}}$, $K_{\mathrm{des}}^{\mathrm{A}}$,
$K_{\mathrm{ads}}^{\mathrm{B}}$, $K_{\mathrm{des}}^{\mathrm{B}}$
are each log-transformed,
with $\theta_f^{\mathrm{sol}}$ left untransformed.
Uniform priors are placed on the transformed parameters over a range
wide relative to the posterior.

Synthetic data are generated using the Newton solver at the true
parameter values
% NOTE: Insert true parameter values once finalised
$\phi^*$,
with dimensionless scan rate $\sigma = 10$ and noise level
$\eta = 0.02$ (the same noise model as in
\cref{ch:hmc,ch:heterogeneous}).

The scan rate is an order of magnitude lower than the $\sigma = 1000$
used for the electron-transfer and heterogeneous reactions. This
reflects the different timescales involved. In the earlier reactions the
processes of interest --- electron transfer and heterogeneous surface
conversion --- are fast, and a high scan rate is needed to drive the
system into the quasi-reversible regime where the current is sensitive
to the kinetic parameters. The adsorption and desorption processes
operate on a slower timescale: they involve the physical attachment and
detachment of molecules from the electrode surface, not just electron
exchange. At $\sigma = 1000$ the potential sweeps too fast for
adsorption to respond, and the voltammogram becomes insensitive to the
adsorption rate constants. Reducing the scan rate to $\sigma = 10$
allows the adsorption/desorption dynamics to compete with diffusion
within the voltammetric window, producing a current response that
carries information about all nine parameters.

\section{Results}\label{sec:ads-results}

\subsection{Solver Verification}\label{sec:ads-verification}

We verify the forward solver against the analytical solution for a
pure monolayer --- a system in which all electroactive material is
pre-adsorbed on the electrode surface and no solution-phase electron
transfer occurs. This situation arises experimentally when an electrode
is modified with a redox-active film (for example, a self-assembled
monolayer of ferrocene-terminated thiols on gold) and the voltammetry
is performed in a blank electrolyte containing no dissolved
electroactive species. Because the adsorbed material is confined to the
surface, no diffusive transport is involved: the current is determined
entirely by the rate at which the finite population of adsorbed
molecules is interconverted by the applied potential.

For a fully reversible adsorbed redox couple the voltammogram is
symmetric about $\theta_f^{\mathrm{ads}}$, with forward and reverse
peaks at the same potential and peak current
\cite{compton2014understanding}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ads-monolayer-peak}
  \frac{I_{\mathrm{peak}}}{FA}
  = \frac{\sigma}{4}\,\Gamma_{\mathrm{total}},
\end{equation}
where $\Gamma_{\mathrm{total}} = \Gamma_\mathrm{A} + \Gamma_\mathrm{B}$
is the total surface coverage. The peak current scales linearly with
the scan rate~$\sigma$, in contrast to the $\sqrt{\sigma}$ dependence
of the solution-phase Randles--\v{S}ev\v{c}\'{\i}k result: the adsorbed
material is finite and does not require diffusive replenishment, so a
faster sweep converts the monolayer more rapidly and produces a
proportionally larger peak. \Cref{fig:ads-verification} confirms that
the solver reproduces both the peak position and peak height predicted
by~\cref{eq:ads-monolayer-peak}.

\subsection{Posterior Distributions}\label{sec:ads-posteriors}

\Cref{fig:ads-corner} shows selected pairwise marginal posteriors
obtained by NUTS and RWMH under equal computational budgets. Both
samplers recover the true parameter values across all nine dimensions,
with the marginals concentrating around the dashed lines. The NUTS and
RWMH contours agree, confirming convergence to the same target
distribution ($\hat{R} < 1.01$ for all parameters).

The off-diagonal panels reveal the correlation structure. As in the
lower-dimensional cases, $\alpha^{\mathrm{sol}}$ and
$K_0^{\mathrm{sol}}$ are correlated due to the kinetic trade-off in
the Butler--Volmer equation. The same correlation appears between
$\alpha^{\mathrm{ads}}$ and $K_0^{\mathrm{ads}}$ for the adsorbed
electron transfer. The adsorption and desorption rate constants for
each species ($K_{\mathrm{ads}}^{j}$ and $K_{\mathrm{des}}^{j}$) are
also correlated: the voltammogram depends on adsorption primarily
through the equilibrium ratio
$K_{\mathrm{ads}}^{j}/K_{\mathrm{des}}^{j}$, so an increase in
$K_{\mathrm{ads}}^{j}$ can be partially compensated by an increase in
$K_{\mathrm{des}}^{j}$. Despite these correlations, both samplers
resolve the individual rate constants.

That both samplers recover the true parameters --- despite the
inference being performed with the backward implicit linearisation
rather than the Newton solver used to generate the data --- confirms
that the approximation~\cref{eq:ads-britz} does not introduce a
systematic bias at the noise level of the synthetic data.

\Cref{fig:ads-current-fit} overlays the voltammogram simulated at the
posterior mean with the voltammogram at the true parameters. The two
curves are indistinguishable, confirming that the posterior mean
provides an accurate reconstruction of the current response.

\subsection{Sampling Efficiency}\label{sec:ads-ess}

\Cref{fig:ads-ess} compares the effective sample size (ESS) of NUTS and
RWMH as a function of the sampling budget. NUTS accumulates independent
samples at a higher rate than RWMH across all nine parameters. The gap
is wider than in the seven-parameter heterogeneous case
(\cref{sec:het-ess}): NUTS achieves roughly four to five times the ESS
of RWMH at the end of the run, compared with three to four times in the
heterogeneous case and approximately two times in the three-parameter
electron-transfer case. This progression is consistent with the known
scaling of random-walk Metropolis, whose optimal acceptance rate
requires step sizes that shrink as $O(d^{-1/2})$, leading to mixing
times that grow polynomially with dimension~$d$. NUTS avoids this
degradation by using gradient information to make large, directed moves
through the parameter space.
