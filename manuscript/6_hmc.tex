\chapter{Hamiltonian Monte Carlo}\label{ch:hmc}

We can generate proposals guided by the posterior geometry with the
adjoint gradients now available. \emph{Hamiltonian Monte
Carlo} (HMC)~\cite{betancourt2018hamiltonian,neal2011mcmc} does this by
borrowing the formalism of Hamiltonian dynamics: the current state of
the Markov chain is treated as a particle that receives a random
momentum kick and then evolves under a Hamiltonian coupling its
position to the posterior. Because the dynamics conserve energy, the
particle traces out long-range trajectories through regions of high
posterior density that would take a random walk many steps to traverse.

\section{The Hamiltonian}\label{sec:hamiltonian}
Let $q \in \mathbb{R}^d$ denote the position (the parameter
  vector~$\phi$; we use $q$ to follow the Hamiltonian mechanics
convention) and $p \in \mathbb{R}^d$ an auxiliary momentum. We define
the Hamiltonian
\begin{equation}
  H(q, p) = U(q) + K(p),
  \label{eq:hamiltonian-decomposition}
\end{equation}
where the potential energy $U(q) = -\ell(q) + \text{const}$ is the
negative log-posterior and the kinetic energy is
\begin{equation}
  K(p) = \tfrac{1}{2}\, p^T M^{-1} p.
  \label{eq:kinetic-energy}
\end{equation}
The mass matrix $M \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ determines the momentum
distribution $p \sim \mathcal{N}(0, M)$; choosing $M$ to approximate the
posterior covariance preconditions the dynamics so that the particle
explores all directions at a similar rate.

\section{Leapfrog Integration and Metropolis
Correction}\label{sec:hmc-algorithm}

Hamilton's equations
\begin{equation}
  \frac{dq}{dt} = M^{-1}p,
  \qquad
  \frac{dp}{dt} = -\nabla_q U(q) = \nabla_q \ell(q)
  \label{eq:hamiltons-equations}
\end{equation}
are integrated numerically using the \emph{leapfrog} integrator, which is
symplectic and time-reversible. Given step size~$\varepsilon$ and
$L$~steps, one trajectory proceeds as
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    p_{i+1/2} &= p_i - \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\,\nabla_q U(q_i), \\
    q_{i+1}   &= q_i + \varepsilon\, M^{-1} p_{i+1/2}, \\
    p_{i+1}   &= p_{i+1/2}
    - \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\,\nabla_q U(q_{i+1}),
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:leapfrog}
\end{equation}
for $i = 0, \ldots, L-1$. Each step requires both a forward and
adjoint solve, with the second gradient reused in the next step.
Because the integrator is symplectic, the energy error
along the trajectory is small and the endpoint $(q^*, p^*)$ is accepted
with probability
\begin{equation}
  r = \min\!\big\{1,\;
  \exp\!\big(H(q, p) - H(q^*, p^*)\big)\big\},
  \label{eq:hmc-acceptance}
\end{equation}
giving high acceptance rates even for distant proposals.

\section{The No-U-Turn Sampler}\label{sec:nuts}

The efficiency of HMC depends on the trajectory length~$L$: too short
and the sampler reverts to near-random-walk behaviour; too long and the
trajectory doubles back, wasting gradient evaluations. The \emph{No-U-Turn
Sampler} (NUTS)~\cite{cabezas2024blackjax,hoffman2014no} eliminates
$L$ as a tuning parameter
by doubling the trajectory in both directions until the endpoints begin
to converge (a U-turn), then drawing a sample uniformly from the valid
states. This leaves only $\varepsilon$ and $M$ to be specified.

\section{Parameter Transformations}\label{sec:transformations}

The leapfrog integrator applies a uniform step size in every coordinate,
so parameters with bounded support or disparate scales require
transformation to an unconstrained space. We apply the logit transform to
the transfer coefficient and the log transform to the rate constant:
\begin{equation}
  \tilde{\alpha} = \operatorname{logit}(\alpha)
  = \log\!\left(\frac{\alpha}{1 - \alpha}\right), \qquad
  \tilde{K}_0 = \log K_0.
  \label{eq:transforms}
\end{equation}
The formal potential $\theta_f$ is already unconstrained. The sampler
operates in $\tilde{\phi} = (\tilde{\alpha},\, \tilde{K}_0,\, \theta_f)$
and the transformations are composed with the forward map so that
reverse-mode AD propagates gradients through them automatically.

\section{Inference Workflow}\label{sec:workflow}

The following four-step pipeline is used for all reactions in this
dissertation. All runtimes were measured on an Apple M1 with eight
cores; no GPU acceleration was used.

\paragraph{Step~1: Diverse initialisation.}
$N_{\mathrm{init}} = 8$ starting points are drawn in the transformed
space via Latin hypercube sampling to ensure broad prior coverage.

\paragraph{Step~2: Gradient-based optimisation to the high-density
region.} Each start is optimised independently with ADAM for a fixed
number of steps. The step budget is chosen to move iterates into the
high-density region of the posterior without collapsing them onto a
single point.

\paragraph{Step~3: Window adaptation.}
Window adaptation~\cite{stan2024reference,cabezas2024blackjax} is run
once, from one of the ADAM endpoints, to tune the leapfrog step
size~$\varepsilon$ via dual averaging and to estimate the mass
matrix~$M$ from the warm-up sample covariance. The tuned
$(\varepsilon,M)$ are shared across all eight NUTS chains. The RWMH
proposal covariance is set to $\Sigma = (2.38^{2}/d)\, M^{-1}$, the
asymptotically optimal scaling for Gaussian targets~\cite{gelman1997weak}.

\paragraph{Step~4: Equal-budget parallel sampling.}
NUTS and RWMH chains are run in parallel from the eight dispersed
ADAM endpoints. Each NUTS iteration costs $L$ leapfrog steps; each
RWMH iteration costs one forward solve. At a matched sampling
wall-time of approximately $20$\,s, we obtain $2{,}400$ NUTS samples
and $24{,}000$ RWMH samples in total.

\subsection{Posterior Distributions}

Both samplers recover good posteriors across the regimes tested below.
This is due to the optimisation stage of \cref{sec:workflow} placing every
chain inside the
high-density region before sampling begins.

\Cref{fig:electron-posteriors} shows the joint and marginal posteriors
from NUTS and RWMH for the quasi-reversible electron-transfer reaction
with $\phi^* = (0.6,\, 10.0,\, 0.5)$ at $\sigma = 1000$
($\Lambda = K_0 / \sqrt{\sigma} \approx 0.32$). Both samplers recover
the true values, with tight marginals in close agreement. The
off-diagonal panels show weak pairwise dependence between all three
parameters, with a mild negative correlation between $K_0$

\subsection{Diagnostics}

Both samplers produce marginals centred on the true values with
similar widths, but this does not establish that the two methods
are equally reliable. Proposals from MCMC methods depend on their
current state and can therefore be correlated. This is an issue since
correlated samples contain less information than independent ones.
Furthermore, we seek some measure of how stable the posterior
distributions are: if we stopped sampling now, would the reported
moments be the same as if we had run for longer?

\paragraph{Effective Sample Size~(ESS)} quantifies the number of
independent draws equivalent to the correlated
chain~\cite{gelman2013bayesian,geyer2011introduction}:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{ESS} = \frac{T}{1 + 2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \rho_k},
  \label{eq:ess}
\end{equation}
where $T$ is the chain length and $\rho_k$ the autocorrelation at
lag~$k$.

When the autocorrelations are predominantly positive,
$\mathrm{ESS} < T$. A sampler can also produce \emph{antithetic}
draws, in which case the denominator
falls below one and $\mathrm{ESS}$
exceeds~$T$~\cite{stan2024reference,vehtari2021rank}. NUTS can exhibit this
behaviour for parameters whose marginal posterior is close to
Gaussian with weak dependence on the remaining
parameters~\cite{stan2024reference}. \Cref{tab:electron-ess} compares
the ESS for NUTS and RWMH. NUTS achieves $1.6$--$1.9\times$ the ESS
of RWMH across all parameters. For~$K_0$, the NUTS ESS exceeds the
chain length ($2834.3 > 2400$) meaning the samples are antithetic.

\paragraph{Gelman--Rubin Statistic~($\hat{R}$)} assesses whether
chains initialised from dispersed starting points have forgotten
those starting points and agree on the posterior. It is the reason
for running multiple chains in parallel initialised from a
different optimised point in Step~4 of \cref{sec:workflow}. Given
$M$~chains of length~$N$, let $W$ denote the mean within-chain
variance and $B$ the between-chain variance of the chain means. The
statistic~\cite{gelman1992inference} is
\begin{equation}
  \hat{R} = \sqrt{\frac{\hat{V}}{W}},
  \qquad
  \hat{V} = \frac{N-1}{N}\,W + \frac{1}{N}\,B,
  \label{eq:rhat}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{V}$ is the pooled estimator of the posterior variance.
When chains have not mixed, $B \gg W$ and $\hat{R} \gg 1$ and on mixing,
$\hat{R} \to 1$. The convention
$\hat{R} < 1.01$~\cite{vehtari2021rank} is adopted as the threshold
below which between-chain disagreement is not distinguishable from
within-chain Monte Carlo variation. While $\hat{R} > 1.01$, reported
moments are biased toward the chains' starting positions and thus not reliable.

\Cref{fig:gr-k0} shows this link for~$K_0$: the NUTS chains
agree from the first checkpoint with $\hat{R} < 1.01$ throughout,
while the RWMH inter-chain initial band is wide associated with $\hat{R}$
at~$1.2$. The chains then tighten their bands over the samples with
$\hat{R}$ crossing the threshold at roughly $0.3$ of the sample proportion.

\Cref{fig:electron-gr} shows that each parameter in the electron
reaction follows a similar pattern: NUTS at $\hat{R} \leq 1.01$ from
the first checkpoint, RWMH crossing the threshold at roughly $30\%$
of the samples for $\alpha$ and $K_0$, but sooner for $\theta_f$. The
long-range NUTS proposals, preconditioned by the window-adapted mass
matrix, leave the initial state after a handful of iterations;
RWMH's local steps retain memory of their starting points for much
longer.

The NUTS curves in \cref{fig:electron-gr} dip below~$1$
early in the run before converging to it. With a well-conditioned
mass matrix NUTS mixes essentially immediately, so $B$ is already
small while $N$ is too small for $W$ to be estimated precisely; the
resulting finite-sample noise can push $\hat{V}$ below $W$ and
$\hat{R}$ below~$1$.
