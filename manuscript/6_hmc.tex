\chapter{Hamiltonian Monte Carlo}\label{ch:hmc}

With the adjoint gradient available, we can generate proposals guided
by the posterior geometry. \emph{Hamiltonian Monte
Carlo} (HMC)~\cite{betancourt2018hamiltonian,neal2011mcmc} does this by
borrowing the formalism of Hamiltonian dynamics: the current state of
the Markov chain is treated as a particle that receives a random
momentum kick and then evolves under a Hamiltonian coupling its
position to the posterior. Because the dynamics conserve energy, the
particle traces out long-range trajectories through regions of high
posterior density that would take a random walk many steps to traverse.

\section{The Hamiltonian}\label{sec:hamiltonian}
Let $q \in \mathbb{R}^d$ denote the position (the parameter
  vector~$\phi$; we use $q$ to follow the Hamiltonian mechanics
convention) and $p \in \mathbb{R}^d$ an auxiliary momentum. We define
the Hamiltonian
\begin{equation}
  H(q, p) = U(q) + K(p),
  \label{eq:hamiltonian-decomposition}
\end{equation}
where the potential energy $U(q) = -\ell(q) + \text{const}$ is the
negative log-posterior and the kinetic energy is
\begin{equation}
  K(p) = \tfrac{1}{2}\, p^T M^{-1} p.
  \label{eq:kinetic-energy}
\end{equation}
The mass matrix $M \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ determines the momentum
distribution $p \sim \mathcal{N}(0, M)$; choosing $M$ to approximate the
posterior covariance preconditions the dynamics so that the particle
explores all directions at a similar rate.

\section{Leapfrog Integration and Metropolis
Correction}\label{sec:hmc-algorithm}

Hamilton's equations
\begin{equation}
  \frac{dq}{dt} = M^{-1}p,
  \qquad
  \frac{dp}{dt} = -\nabla_q U(q) = \nabla_q \ell(q)
  \label{eq:hamiltons-equations}
\end{equation}
are integrated numerically using the \emph{leapfrog} integrator, which is
symplectic and time-reversible. Given step size~$\varepsilon$ and
$L$~steps, one trajectory proceeds as
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    p_{i+1/2} &= p_i - \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\,\nabla_q U(q_i), \\
    q_{i+1}   &= q_i + \varepsilon\, M^{-1} p_{i+1/2}, \\
    p_{i+1}   &= p_{i+1/2}
    - \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\,\nabla_q U(q_{i+1}),
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:leapfrog}
\end{equation}
for $i = 0, \ldots, L-1$. Each step requires one gradient evaluation via
the adjoint method of \cref{ch:adjoint}, costing one forward and one
adjoint solve. Because the integrator is symplectic, the energy error
along the trajectory is small and the endpoint $(q^*, p^*)$ is accepted
with probability
\begin{equation}
  r = \min\!\big\{1,\;
  \exp\!\big(H(q, p) - H(q^*, p^*)\big)\big\},
  \label{eq:hmc-acceptance}
\end{equation}
giving high acceptance rates even for distant proposals.

\section{The No-U-Turn Sampler}\label{sec:nuts}

The efficiency of HMC depends on the trajectory length~$L$: too short
and the sampler reverts to near-random-walk behaviour; too long and the
trajectory doubles back, wasting gradient evaluations. The \emph{No-U-Turn
Sampler} (NUTS)~\cite{cabezas2024blackjax,hoffman2014no} eliminates
$L$ as a tuning parameter
by doubling the trajectory in both directions until the endpoints begin
to converge (a U-turn), then drawing a sample uniformly from the valid
states. This leaves only $\varepsilon$ and $M$ to be specified.

\section{Parameter Transformations}\label{sec:transformations}

The leapfrog integrator applies a uniform step size in every coordinate,
so parameters with bounded support or disparate scales require
transformation to an unconstrained space. We apply the logit transform to
the transfer coefficient and the log transform to the rate constant:
\begin{equation}
  \tilde{\alpha} = \operatorname{logit}(\alpha)
  = \log\!\left(\frac{\alpha}{1 - \alpha}\right), \qquad
  \tilde{K}_0 = \log K_0.
  \label{eq:transforms}
\end{equation}
The formal potential $\theta_f$ is already unconstrained. The sampler
operates in $\tilde{\phi} = (\tilde{\alpha},\, \tilde{K}_0,\, \theta_f)$
and the transformations are composed with the forward map so that
reverse-mode AD propagates gradients through them automatically.

\section{Inference Workflow}\label{sec:workflow}

The following four-step pipeline is used for all reactions in this
dissertation.

\paragraph{Step~1: Diverse initialisation.}
$N_{\mathrm{init}} = 8$ starting points (matching the available CPU
cores) are drawn in the transformed space via Latin hypercube sampling
to ensure broad prior coverage.

\paragraph{Step~2: Gradient-based optimisation.}
Each start is optimised independently with ADAM for a fixed number of
steps to move them to high density areas in the posterior space.

\paragraph{Step~3: Window adaptation.}
A NUTS warmup is then run from point with the highest likelihood.
This consists of $N_{\mathrm{adapt}}$ iterations tunes $\varepsilon$
via dual averaging~\cite{hoffman2014no} and estimates $M$ from the
warm-up sample covariance. The same covariance sets the RWMH proposal
$\Sigma$, scaled to achieve~$\approx 23\%$ acceptance~\cite{gelman1997weak}.

\paragraph{Step~4: Equal-budget sampling.}
Chains of NUTS and RWMH are run from the same initial (optimised)
states. Since each NUTS iteration requires $L$ leapfrog steps while
each RWMH iteration costs one forward solve. Because NUTS selects its
trajectory length adaptively, the total budget is measured after
sampling and the RWMH chain length set to match. For the
electron-transfer reaction this gives $2{,}400$ NUTS samples and
$24{,}000$ RWMH samples, each requiring approximately $20$~seconds of
sampling time.

All runtimes in this dissertation were measured on an Apple M1 with
eight cores; no GPU acceleration was used.

\subsection{Effective Sample Size}
Successive MCMC draws are generally correlated. Positive
autocorrelation, which is typical of random-walk proposals, means that
each draw carries less information than an independent sample. The
\emph{effective sample size}
(ESS)~\cite{gelman2013bayesian,geyer2011introduction} quantifies the
number of independent draws equivalent to the correlated chain:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{ESS} = \frac{T}{1 + 2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \rho_k},
  \label{eq:ess}
\end{equation}
where $T$ is the chain length and $\rho_k$ the autocorrelation at
lag~$k$. When the autocorrelations are predominantly positive,
$\mathrm{ESS} < T$. However, a sampler can also produce
\emph{antithetic} (negatively autocorrelated) draws, in which case the
denominator falls below one and $\mathrm{ESS}$ exceeds~$T$: the
anticorrelated draws reduce the variance of posterior mean estimates
below what independent sampling would
achieve~\cite{stan2024reference,vehtari2021rank}. NUTS can exhibit this
behaviour for parameters whose marginal posterior is close to Gaussian
with weak dependence on other
parameters~\cite{stan2024reference}.

\Cref{fig:electron-ess} compares ESS growth over the sampling budget for
NUTS and RWMH on a normalised sampling axis. NUTS accumulates
effective samples at a higher rate across all three parameters.
\Cref{tab:electron-ess} reports the final values: NUTS achieves
$1.5$--$2.4\times$ the ESS of RWMH for all parameters. For~$K_0$, the
NUTS ESS exceeds the chain length ($2986.7 > 2400$), consistent with the
antithetic behaviour described above; in the transformed space,
$\log K_0$ has an approximately Gaussian marginal with weak dependence on
the remaining parameters, as visible in
\cref{fig:electron-posteriors}. As the dimension grows, a random
proposal of fixed covariance is increasingly likely to land in a region
of low posterior density. The proposal covariance must therefore shrink
to maintain a reasonable acceptance rate, and each accepted proposal
covers less of the parameter space. HMC avoids this by using the
gradient to follow the contours of the posterior rather than stepping
blindly. We quantify this scaling in the following chapters.
