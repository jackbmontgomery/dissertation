\chapter{Electrochemical Systems}\label{ch:electrochem}

In a typical electrochemical experiment, a \emph{potentiostat} applies a
controlled, time-varying potential to a \emph{working electrode} immersed
in an electrolyte solution and records the resulting current
(\cref{fig:electrochemical-cell}). A reference electrode provides a
stable potential baseline and a counter electrode completes the
electrical circuit.

\begin{figure}
  \centering
  \input{figures/electrochemical-cell.tex}
  \caption[Schematic diagram of a three-electrode electrochemical
  cell]{Schematic diagram of a three-electrode electrochemical cell. The
    potentiostat controls the potential difference between the working
    and reference electrodes whilst driving current (red) through the
    circuit formed by the working and counter electrodes via the
  electrolyte solution.}
  \label{fig:electrochemical-cell}
\end{figure}

The reaction at the electrode surface depends on the applied potential and
the species in solution. In the simplest case, a solution-phase species
undergoes a single-electron redox reaction:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:redox}
  \mathrm{A} + \mathrm{e}^{-} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{B},
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{A}$ gains one electron (reduction) to form $\mathrm{B}$;
and $\mathrm{B}$ loses an electron (oxidation) to form $\mathrm{A}$.

\section{Voltammetry}

Voltammetry is the class of techniques in which the potential applied to
the working electrode is varied and the resulting current recorded. The
current--potential curve, called a \emph{voltammogram}, encodes
information about both the reaction kinetics and the transport of species
in solution.

In a cyclic voltammetry (CV) experiment the potential is swept linearly
from a starting value~$E_i$, where no reaction occurs, to a vertex
potential~$E_v$ where conversion between species occurs, and then
back to~$E_i$. The current $I$ is recorded throughout. An example of
the triangular waveform and the resulting voltammogram are shown in
\cref{fig:potential-voltammogram}.

The forward sweep produces a reduction (cathodic) peak; the return sweep
produces an oxidation (anodic) peak~\footnote{Following IUPAC
  recommendations for sign conventions of
  electrochemical data, positive values will be assigned to anodic currents.
}. The peak shape arises from
competition between two rate-limiting processes. Early in the forward
sweep the driving force for reduction is small and negligible current
flows. As the potential moves to more negative values the current
increases: the system is under \emph{kinetic control}, with the rate of
electron transfer as the limiting step. Eventually species $\mathrm{A}$ near the
electrode is depleted and the current peaks. Beyond this point the
current decreases despite the applied potential increasing.
This is due to the system passing into \emph{diffusional control}:
diffusive transport of fresh $\mathrm{A}$ to the surface becomes the
limiting factor.

\begin{figure}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/2-voltammogram.png}
  \end{center}
  \caption[Applied potential and voltammogram]{Left: the triangular
    potential waveform applied in a cyclic voltammetry experiment. Right:
    the resulting voltammogram, showing the cathodic
    (reduction) peak on the forward sweep and the anodic
  (oxidation) peak on the reverse sweep.}
  \label{fig:potential-voltammogram}
\end{figure}

The peak positions and separation between anodic and cathodic peaks
carry information about the electrode kinetics. The \emph{scan rate} $\nu$
$(\mathrm{V\,s^{-1}})$ controls the timescale
of the experiment: faster scans probe faster kinetic processes, while
slower scans allow more time for diffusion and can reveal coupled chemical
reactions. The scan rate is chosen for each reaction mechanism so that the
current response is sensitive to the target parameters; the rationale for
each choice is given when the mechanism is introduced.

\section{Mathematical Description}

We consider a planar disc electrode of radius~$r_e$ which is much
larger than the diffusion length on the timescale of the experiment. This
assumption allows us to model only the diffusion perpendicular to the
surface of the electrode reducing the problem to one spatial
dimension. (\cref{fig:electrode-reaction}).

\begin{figure}
  \centering
  \input{figures/electrode-reaction.tex}
  \caption[One-dimensional electrode reaction]{Schematic of the
    one-dimensional electrode reaction. Species
    $\mathrm{A}$ diffuses from the bulk solution towards the electrode
    surface, where it is reduced ($+e^-$) to form species $\mathrm{B}$.
    The reverse oxidation ($-e^-$) converts $\mathrm{B}$ back to
    $\mathrm{A}$. Species $\mathrm{B}$ diffuses away from the electrode
  into the bulk. Each processes also happens in reverse.}
  \label{fig:electrode-reaction}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Diffusion}\label{sec:diffusion}

Transport in unstirred solution is governed by Fick's second
law~\cite{crank1979mathematics}:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial c_j}{\partial t}
  = D_j \frac{\partial^2 c_j}{\partial x^2}, \qquad j \in \{A, B\},
  \label{eq:diffusion}
\end{equation}
where $D_j$ $(\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}})$ is the diffusion coefficient and
$x$ is the distance from the electrode surface. The domain is
semi-infinite, $x \geq 0$.

\subsection{Electrode Kinetics}\label{sec:electrode-kinetics}

The diffusive flux of species $j$ at a point $x^*$ is given by Fick's
first law~\cite{crank1979mathematics}:
\begin{equation}
  j_{x^*} = -D_j\left(\frac{\partial c_j}{\partial x}\right)\bigg|_{x = x^*}.
  \label{eq:ficks-first}
\end{equation}

The rate of reaction~\cref{eq:redox} at the electrode surface is governed
by the Butler--Volmer equation~\cite{dickinson2020butler}:
\begin{equation}
  j_{0, A} = k_0 \left[ c_A(t,0)\,
    e^{-\alpha \frac{F}{\mathcal{R}\mathcal{T}} (E(t) - E^0_f)}
    - c_B(t,0)\,
    e^{(1-\alpha) \frac{F}{\mathcal{R}\mathcal{T}} (E(t) - E^0_f)}
  \right]
  \label{eq:butler-volmer}
\end{equation}
where $k_0$ $(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}})$ is the standard rate constant, $\alpha
\in (0,1)$ is the transfer coefficient, $F$ is Faraday's constant,
$\mathcal{R}$ is the universal gas constant, and $\mathcal{T}$ is the
absolute temperature.

Combining \cref{eq:ficks-first,eq:butler-volmer} at $x = 0$ gives a
Neumann boundary condition:
\begin{equation}
  -D_A\left(\frac{\partial c_A}{\partial x}\right)\bigg|_{x = 0} =
  k_0 \left[ c_A(t,0)\,
    e^{-\alpha \frac{F}{\mathcal{R}\mathcal{T}} (E(t) - E^0_f)}
    - c_B(t,0)\,
    e^{(1-\alpha) \frac{F}{\mathcal{R}\mathcal{T}} (E(t) - E^0_f)}
  \right]
  \label{eq:electrode-boundary-condition}
\end{equation}
Stoichiometric conservation at the surface requires
\begin{equation}
  D_B\left(\frac{\partial c_B}{\partial x}\right)\bigg|_{x = 0} =
  -D_A\left(\frac{\partial c_A}{\partial x}\right)\bigg|_{x = 0}.
  \label{eq:mass-conservation}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Boundary and Initial Conditions}

Initially both species are at their bulk concentrations:
\begin{equation}
  c_A(0, x) = c_A^*, \qquad c_B(0, x) = c_B^*.
  \label{eq:initial-conditions}
\end{equation}
Far from the electrode the bulk values are recovered:
\begin{equation}
  c_j(t, x) \to c_j^* \quad \text{as } x \to \infty, \qquad j \in \{A, B\}.
  \label{eq:infinite-boundary-condition}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Current}

The measured current is
\begin{equation}
  I = F A_{\mathrm{el}}\, j_{0, A}
  \label{eq:current}
\end{equation}
where $A_{\mathrm{el}} = \pi r_e^2$ is the electrode area and $j_{0,A}$
is the surface flux of $\mathrm{A}$ from \cref{eq:ficks-first}.

\section{Non-Dimensionalisation}\label{sec:nondim}

\Cref{eq:diffusion,eq:electrode-boundary-condition,eq:mass-conservation,%
eq:initial-conditions,eq:infinite-boundary-condition} constitute an
initial-boundary value problem. We non-dimensionalise by scaling
concentrations by $c_A^*$, diffusion coefficients by $D_A$, lengths by
$r_e$, and potentials by the thermal voltage $\mathcal{R}\mathcal{T}/F$.
\Cref{tab:normalisation} summarises the scalings.

\begin{table}[ht]
  \centering
  \begin{tabular*}{\textwidth}{@{\extracolsep{\fill}}llll@{}}
    \hline

    diffusion coefficient & $d_j = D_j / D_{\mathrm{A}}$ & rate
    constant & $K_0 = k_0\,r_e / D_{\mathrm{A}}$ \\
    spatial coordinate & $X = x / r_e$ & scan rate & $\sigma = (r_e^2
    / D_{\mathrm{A}})(F / \mathcal{R}\mathcal{T})\,\nu$ \\
    time & $T = D_{\mathrm{A}} t / r_e^2$ & current & $J = I / (\pi
    r_e F D_{\mathrm{A}} c_{\mathrm{A}}^* )$ \\
    applied potential & $\theta = (F / \mathcal{R}\mathcal{T})\,E$ & & \\
    \hline
  \end{tabular*}

  normalisation of variables.}

Under these scalings the governing equations become
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial C_j}{\partial T} = d_j \frac{\partial^2 C_j}{\partial X^2},
  \label{eq:nd_diffusion}
\end{equation}
with initial conditions $C_A(0, X) = 1$, $C_B(0, X) = C_B^*$.
The
semi-infinite domain is truncated at $X_{\max} =
6\sqrt{T_{\mathrm{max}}}$ and $T_{\mathrm{max}} = 2(\theta_v -
\theta_i)/\sigma$. This truncation is  chosen so that
$X_{\max}$ exceeds the diffusion length
$\sqrt{2T_{\mathrm{max}}}$~\cite{einstein1905brownian} by a wide
margin~\cite{svir2003solution},
and the far-field conditions $C_A = 1$, $C_B = C_B^*$ are imposed there.

The dimensionless Butler--Volmer boundary condition and mass conservation
at $X = 0$ are
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    \frac{\partial C_A}{\partial X}\bigg|_{X=0} &=
    K_0 \left[ C_A(T,0)\, e^{-\alpha(\theta(T) - \theta_f)}
    - C_B(T,0)\, e^{(1-\alpha)(\theta(T) - \theta_f)} \right], \\
    d_B \frac{\partial C_B}{\partial X}\bigg|_{X=0} &=
    -\frac{\partial C_A}{\partial X}\bigg|_{X=0}.
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:nondim-electrode-boundary}
\end{equation}

The dimensionless current is
\begin{equation}
  J_{0,A} = -\left(\frac{\partial C_A}{\partial X}\right)\bigg|_{X=0},
  \label{eq:nd_flux}
\end{equation}
so that $I = \pi r_e F D_A c_A^* J_{0,A}$. A single dimensionless
simulation applies to any combination of $c_A^*$, $D_A$, and $r_e$.

\section{Equal Diffusion Coefficients}

For the remainder of this dissertation we assume $d_B = 1$. Since both
species then satisfy the same diffusion equation with equal and opposite
surface fluxes (\cref{eq:nondim-electrode-boundary}), the sum
$C_A + C_B$ satisfies a diffusion equation with homogeneous Neumann
boundary conditions and constant initial and far-field data.
It follows that $C_A + C_B = 1 + C_B^*$ everywhere. Writing $C \equiv
C_A$, so that $C_B = 1 + C_B^* - C$, the problem reduces to a single PDE
with electrode boundary condition
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial C}{\partial X}\bigg|_{X=0} =
  K_0 \left[ C(T,0)\, e^{-\alpha(\theta(T) - \theta_f)}
  - (1 + C_B^* - C(T,0))\, e^{(1-\alpha)(\theta(T) - \theta_f)} \right].
  \label{eq:equal_diff_bc}
\end{equation}

For notational convenience in the discretisation that follows, we define
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    K_{\text{red}} &= K_0 \exp\bigg[-\alpha(\theta(T) - \theta_f)\bigg], \\
    K_{\text{ox}} &= K_0 \exp\bigg[(1-\alpha)(\theta(T) - \theta_f)\bigg].
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:kred_kox}
\end{equation}
