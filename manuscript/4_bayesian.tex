\chapter{Bayesian Approach to the Inverse Problem}\label{ch:bayesian}

The correlations and negligible impact of parameters discussed in
\cref{sec:bv-effects} make the inverse problem ill-posed: a unique,
stable solution may not exist. We therefore take a Bayesian approach and
seek probability distributions over the parameters rather than point
estimates.

\section{Noise Model}\label{sec:noise-model}

We model the observed dimensionless flux as the forward map corrupted
by Gaussian noise:
\begin{equation}
  y_i = \mathcal{P}(\phi)_i + \varepsilon_i,
  \qquad \varepsilon_i \overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}
  \mathcal{N}(0,\, \varsigma^2),
  \label{eq:noise-model}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{y} = (y_1, \ldots, y_N)$ are the $N$ flux measurements
and $\phi = (\alpha,\, K_0,\, \theta_f)$. The noise standard deviation is
set as a fraction of the peak flux magnitude:
\begin{equation}
  \varsigma = \eta \, \big\lvert J_{\mathrm{peak}} \big\rvert,
  \qquad
  J_{\mathrm{peak}} = \min_{i}\, \mathcal{P}(\phi^*)_i,
  \label{eq:noise-level}
\end{equation}
where $\eta \in (0,1)$ is a dimensionless noise parameter and $\phi^*$
is the true parameter vector. Experimental measurements typically exhibit
noise levels of $1$--$2\%$ of the peak
current~\cite{morris2013comparison}, corresponding to $\eta \in [0.01,\,
0.02]$. For a given $\eta$, the noise level $\varsigma$ is determined by
the noiseless forward solve and is treated as a known constant throughout
the inference. Examples of synthetic noisy data are shown in
\cref{fig:noisy-samples}.

\section{Posterior Distribution}\label{sec:posterior}

Bayes' theorem gives
\begin{equation}
  p(\phi \mid \mathbf{y})
  = \frac{L(\mathbf{y} \mid \phi)\, \pi(\phi)}{p(\mathbf{y})},
  \label{eq:bayes}
\end{equation}
where $L(\mathbf{y} \mid \phi)$ is the likelihood,
$\pi(\phi)$ is the prior, and
$p(\mathbf{y})$ is the evidence. We adopt a uniform prior over
physically reasonable bounds ($\alpha \in (0,1)$, $K_0 > 0$), so that
\begin{equation}
  p(\phi \mid \mathbf{y}) \propto L(\mathbf{y} \mid \phi).
  \label{eq:posterior-proportional}
\end{equation}
Under the noise model~\cref{eq:noise-model} the observations are
conditionally independent given $\phi$. Dropping constant terms that do
not depend on $\phi$, the log-likelihood is
\begin{equation}
  \ell(\phi)
  = -\frac{1}{2\varsigma^2}
  \sum_{i=1}^{N}\big(y_i - \mathcal{P}(\phi)_i\big)^2.
  \label{eq:log-likelihood}
\end{equation}

\section{Random Walk Metropolis--Hastings}\label{sec:rwmh}

The posterior~\cref{eq:posterior-proportional} is known only up to a
normalising constant. Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) addresses this by
constructing a chain whose stationary distribution is the posterior.

In the random walk Metropolis--Hastings (RWMH) algorithm
\cite{hastings1970monte,cabezas2024blackjax}, proposals are
drawn from a symmetric Gaussian centred at the current state:
\begin{equation}
  \phi_{\mathrm{cand}} \sim
  \mathcal{N}(\phi^{(t)},\, \Sigma).
  \label{eq:rwmh-proposal}
\end{equation}
Proposals are accepted or rejected according to the
Metropolis--Hastings acceptance probability~\cite{hastings1970monte}
\begin{equation}
  r = \min\!\left\{
    \exp\!\big(\ell(\phi_{\mathrm{cand}}) - \ell(\phi^{(t)})\big),\; 1
  \right\}.
  \label{eq:rwmh-acceptance}
\end{equation}
The proposal covariance $\Sigma$ is tuned to achieve an acceptance rate
of approximately $23\%$, which is asymptotically optimal for Gaussian
targets~\cite{gelman1997weak}.

\subsection{Sampling Results}\label{sec:sampling-results}

We apply RWMH to synthetic data generated with true parameters
$\phi^* = (0.6,\, 10.0,\, 0.5)$ at scan rate $\sigma = 1000$ and noise
level $\eta = 0.02$. \Cref{fig:rwmh-hist} shows the marginal posterior
histograms which confirm all three distributions concentrate around
the true values (dashed lines).

\begin{figure}[ht]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/4-rwmh-hist.png}
  \caption[Marginal posterior histograms from RWMH sampling]{Marginal
    posterior histograms for the three Butler--Volmer
    parameters obtained by RWMH sampling on synthetic data with noise
    level $\eta = 0.02$. Red dashed lines indicate the true parameter
  values used to generate the data.}
  \label{fig:rwmh-hist}
\end{figure}

The histograms exhibit extended tails originating from the chain's
early samples, visible in \cref{fig:rwmh-burn-in}. The chain spends
many iterations traversing low-probability areas before settling near
the true values. This is due to it being initialised far from the
high-density region. These initial samples are typically discarded as
\emph{burn-in}. But since each likelihood evaluation requires a full
forward solve, this burn-in represents a significant waste of computation.

A gradient of the log-likelihood would address this. We could locate
the high-density region by optimisation before sampling begins.
Gradient-free optimisers could do the same, but gradient-based methods
offer faster and more robust convergence. Moreover, gradients can be
incorporated into the MCMC proposal itself, yielding samplers that
explore the posterior more efficiently than random walks. Both uses
require differentiating through the forward map~$\mathcal{P}$, which
we develop in the following chapter.
